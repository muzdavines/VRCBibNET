{Read Jeremiah 29:1–7}
1. God Knows Their Address
Jeremiah 29:1 says: “These are the words of the letter that Jeremiah the prophet sent from Jerusalem to the surviving elders of the exiles, and to the priests, the prophets, and all the people, whom Nebuchadnezzar had taken into exile from Jerusalem to Babylon.”

Nebuchadnezzar had taken them.
He had armies.  political power military strength.
From the human point of view, Babylon had won.
But then verse 4 says: “Thus says the LORD of hosts, the God of Israel, to all the exiles whom I have sent into exile from Jerusalem to Babylon.”

Nebuchadnezzar had taken them, but God says, “I have sent you.”
That does not remove Neb’s guilt. Babylon remains violent, arrogant and idolatrous.

But Babylon is not sovereign.

Neb ¬ overpowered the Lord. G ¬ been forced out of Jerusalem. Exile has not happened because the Babylonian gods were stronger than the God of Israel.

The exile is taking place under the sovereign judgment of God.

That is difficult comfort, but it is still comfort.
If Babylon were completely outside God’s control, the exiles would have no hope at all.
But even in a situation they did not choose, God remained Lord.
They were not outside His knowledge / His reach / His purposes.
Their address had changed, but their God had not.

There are times when life takes us somewhere we did not choose.
We may find ourselves living with:

.     work that feels frustrating

You may not understand why you are there. / know how long the season will last / not yet see what God is doing.
But the Lord has not misplaced you.
The God of Israel was still God in Babylon.
And the God and Father of our Lord Jesus Christ is still God in the places we would never have chosen for ourselves.

2. Unpack the Boxes
God’s first instruction to the exiles is surprising.

Jer 29:5–6: “Build houses and live in them; PLANT GARDENS and eat their produce. Take wives and have sons and daughters; take wives for your sons, and give your daughters in marriage, that they may bear sons and daughters; multiply there, and do not decrease.”

The people wanted God to tell them to pack. -U-Haul
Instead, God tells them to unpack. Build houses. Plant gardens. Raise families. Continue living.
¬ what they wanted to hear.
They wanted the exile to be a brief interruption. God tells them it will be a long season.
A garden is not planted for tomorrow morning ~ plant because you expect to remain long enough to eat the fruit.
A house is not a tent. It implies some permanence.
God is telling them: “Do not waste the years I have placed before you.”
There was a temptation for the exiles to put life on hold.
They might say: “We will serve God properly when we return to Jerusalem.”, “We will build when we get home.”, “We will raise families when circumstances improve.”, “We will become fruitful once Babylon is behind us.”
But God says they are to live faithfully now.
Faithfulness cannot be postponed until life becomes ideal.

**Settle down without settling for Babylon**
BALANCE.
The exiles were to settle down in Babylon, but they were not to settle for Babylon.
They were to build houses, but Babylon was not their true home.
They were to work and raise children, but they were not to worship Babylon’s gods.
They were to participate in the life of the city, but they were not to become spiritually indistinguishable from the city.
They were temporary residents, not permanent citizens.

That tension continues in the New Testament.
Peter addresses Christians as: “sojourners and exiles” 1 Pet 2:11
Heb 13:14 “For here we have no lasting city, but we seek the city that is to come.”
Christians are not called to abandon ordinary life. We work. We marry. We raise families.
We study. We build. We create. We serve our neighbours.
But we do all those things knowing that this world, in its present fallen form, is not our final home.
~Application~
Some believers spend so much time longing to escape the circumstances that they stop being faithful within.
They think: “When this difficulty ends, I will serve God.” “When I get a better job, I will be generous.” “When my family situation changes, I will become more patient.” “When my health improves, I will be useful.” “When the church becomes exactly as I want it, I will participate.”
But God often calls us to plant gardens in Babylon.
Do the next faithful thing: Pray today. Serve today. Love the person in front of you today. Use the gifts God has given you today.

Babylon may be your present address, but that does not mean your years there must be spiritually barren.

3. Pray for the Street Where You Live - NO SLIDE
Verse 7 goes further: “But seek the welfare of the city where I have sent you into exile, and pray to the LORD on its behalf, for in its welfare you will find your welfare.”
This would have been shocking. The exiles were not merely to tolerate Babylon.
They were to seek its welfare.
The word translated “welfare” is the Hebrew word shalom.
It can carry the idea of peace, wholeness, prosperity and well-being.
God tells them to seek the shalom of Babylon.
Not its idolatry /  arrogance /  military ambition.
But the well-being of the people and the city around them.
And they are to pray for it.

Prayer for Babylon would not come naturally.
Babylon had taken them from their homes / represented humiliation and loss / the enemy.
Yet God commands His people to pray for the place of their exile.

The exiles were not to respond in one of two wrong ways.
i. They were not to assimilate, becoming just like Babylon.
ii. But neither were they to isolate themselves, becoming bitter, useless and hostile toward everyone around them.
They were to live as a faithful presence.
They were to be good neighbours at their temporary address.

-The church in the world-
This helps us understand the place of the church in society.
The church is not Babylon.
The church must not absorb the world’s values, repeat its lies or worship its idols.
But neither are Christians called to sit at a distance, hoping society collapses around us.
We are to pray for our communities.

We should want [children to be safe | justice to be done |families to flourish |  the lonely to be cared for | want honest government | want good schools, good work and peaceful streets.]
Paul tells Timothy: “I urge that supplications, prayers, intercessions, and thanksgivings be made for all people, for kings and all who are in high positions.” 1 Tim 2:1–2

That command was not written when Christian rulers occupied every throne.
God’s people pray for those around them because our God is merciful.

~Application~
Do we pray for the place where we live?
It is easy to complain about our town, our nation, our politicians, our neighbours and our culture.
But do we pray? Do we seek the welfare of the street where God has placed us?
Christians should be among the most useful, dependable and prayerful people in any community.  Not because Babylon is our home.
But because God has placed us here for this season.

4. Do Not Trust a False Removal Date
Verses 8–9  “For thus says the LORD of hosts, the God of Israel: Do not let your prophets and your diviners who are among you deceive you, and do not listen to the dreams that they dream, for it is a lie that they are prophesying to you in my name; I did not send them, declares the LORD.”
False prophets were offering a more attractive message.

Jeremiah said the exile would be long.
The false prophets said it would be brief. They were effectively telling the people:
“Do not unpack.” “Do not build.” “Do not plant.” “The removal van is already on its way.” Uhaul

Their message sounded hopeful, but it was a false hope.
It gave the people what they wanted to hear, but not what God had said.
False hope is dangerous because it often sounds more encouraging than the truth.

It offers:
.      escape without endurance
.      blessing without repentance
.      restoration without discipline
.      promise without patience
.      victory without the cross

The false prophets were speaking in God’s name.
That made the deception particularly serious.
They were not saying, “Forget God.” or  claiming, “God told us.”
Yet God says: “I did not send them.”

~Application~
We still need discernment. Not every optimistic message is biblical hope.
There are voices that promise:
.      immediate breakthrough
.      guaranteed prosperity
.      certain healing
.      effortless victory
.      success for every plan
.      a rapid end to every painful season

Sometimes God delivers quickly | He heals immediately | opens a door no one expected]
But Jeremiah 29 warns us not to build faith upon a timetable God has not promised.
Biblical hope does not depend on God doing what I want by the date I have chosen.
Biblical hope says: “Even if this season is longer than I expected, God will remain faithful.”
False prophets gave the people an easy removal date.
God gave them something better. He gave them His word.

5. God Keeps the Calendar
Jer29:10 says: “For thus says the LORD: When seventy years are completed for Babylon, I will visit you, and I will fulfill to you my promise and bring you back to this place.”
The exile had a limit. 70 years.

Both comforting and difficult.
comforting ~ Babylon would not last forever.
difficult ~ seventy years is a lifetime.
The original hearers would never return personally to Jerusalem.
They would build houses in Babylon and die in Babylon.
Their children or grandchildren would see the promise fulfilled.
God’s plan was larger than the individual lifespan of each exile.
That is important for us.

We sometimes assume that if God is faithful, we must personally see every result.
But some believers sow what another generation will reap.
Some pray for years before the answer appears.
Some work faithfully without seeing the final fruit.
Some teach children who will only understand the significance decades later.
Some help restore a church but do not live to see what that church later becomes.
Faith believes that God’s work does not end when our part in it ends.

But He has shown us who He is.


6. A Future and a Hope
Then comes the best-known verse in the chapter.
Jer 29:11: “For I know the plans I have for you, declares the LORD, plans for welfare and not for evil, to give you a future and a hope.”

These words are often removed from Babylon. They are placed on graduation cards, calendars and motivational posters.
They are sometimes treated as though God were saying: “I will make every individual plan succeed.” “You will get the career you want.” “You will avoid disappointment.” “Your immediate future will be comfortable.”

But the first people who received this promise were exiles facing seventy years in Babylon.
God’s plans were not Hananiah’s plans. Hananiah promised escape without waiting. God promised restoration through seventy years of exile. The hope of verse 11 is not that suffering will end tomorrow. It is that suffering will not have the final word.
Some of them would never see Jerusalem again.This is not a promise of immediate personal success.It is a promise of God’s covenant faithfulness.
The “you” is addressed to God’s exiled people.
God is saying: “Babylon will not cancel My purposes.”
 “Judgment will not destroy the promise.”
“I have not forgotten Abraham, Isaac, Jacob or David.”
“I will preserve My people.”
“I will bring them home.”
“I will continue the plan of redemption.”

The verse becomes stronger when it remains in context.
God is not promising that the exiles will avoid Babylon.
They are already there. He is promising that Babylon will not have the final word.
“I KNOW!”
Notice where the confidence rests: “For I KNOW the plans I have for you.”
The people don’t know [||how life in Babylon will unfold | know which houses they will build | which children will eventually return | how God will preserve His promises.
But God knows.!!
Their hope does not rest in their understanding of the plan. It rests in the God who knows the plan.

~The underside of the tapestry~
Life can resemble looking at the underside of a tapestry. : there are knots, loose threads, strange colours and lines that seem to go nowhere.
The person looking from below may wonder whether there is any design at all.
But the maker sees the other side. God sees the pattern.
We see the temporary address. God sees the journey home.
We see the seventy years. God sees the return.
We see the exile. God sees the coming of Christ.

~Not less personal~
Understanding Jeremiah 29:11 corporately does not make it less personal.
It prevents us from making it individualistic and shallow.
The God who preserved His covenant people is the God who holds every individual believer in Christ.

Our hope is not that every earthly plan will succeed.
Our hope is that nothing can separate us from the love of God in Christ Jesus.
Our circumstances may be painful. 
Our earthly future may contain things we would never choose.

But for those who are in Christ, the final destination is secure.
Babylon may be the present address.
It is not the final home.

7. The Greatest Promise Is God Himself
J 29:12–14 says: “Then you will call upon me and come and pray to me, and I will hear you. You will seek me and find me, when you seek me with all your heart. I will be found by you, declares the LORD, and I will restore your fortunes and gather you from all the nations and all the places where I have driven you, declares the LORD, and I will bring you back to the place from which I sent you into exile.”
The promise is not merely that they will find Jerusalem again.
They will find God. “You will seek me and find me.”

That is the heart of restoration.

The nation HAD wanted God’s gifts without God.

They wanted:
.     the land without covenant faithfulness
.     the temple without holiness
.     protection without obedience
.     blessing without repentance
.     worship ceremonies without wholehearted devotion

God had removed the gifts they had turned into idols. But His purpose was not simply to punish them.
It was to draw them back to Himself.
The exile would expose their false securities.
They could no longer point to the temple and assume they were safe.
They could no longer trust in the city walls.
They could no longer rely upon the land while ignoring the Lord of the land.

In Babylon, stripped of their familiar securities, they must seek God.

Augustine famously prayed: “You have made us for yourself, and our heart is restless until it rests in you.”

The deepest human exile is not geographical. It is alienation from God.
And the greatest homecoming is not entering a city. It is being reconciled to Him.

~Application~
We often ask God to change our address when God is first dealing with our hearts.
We say: “Get me out of this situation.”,  “Remove this pressure.”, “Restore what I lost.”
“Take me back to the way things were.”, God may do those things.

But sometimes His deeper purpose is: “Seek Me here.”

It is possible to leave Babylon geographically and still carry Babylon in the heart.
It is possible for circumstances to improve while the soul remains far from God.

The greatest promise in Jeremiah 29 is not merely: “You will go home.”
It is: “You will seek me and find me.”

Conclusion: Christ Came to Our Address
Jeremiah 29 is ¬ a message about remaining hopeful when life is difficult.
The deepest problem in the chapter is sin.
¬ innocent travellers who had accidentally taken a wrong turn.

They were in exile because they had rejected God.

They had enjoyed His land while resisting His rule, entered His temple while worshipping idols, heard His prophets but refused to listen.
Babylon was not the disease. Babylon was the consequence.
And that is our condition too.

The Bible says that all humanity is living in exile from God.
We were made for His presence, but we have turned away from Him.

We have tried to build homes for ourselves in things that cannot last:
.     Money,  success, pleasure, religion, reputation, relationships
.     political causes, personal identity, our own goodness

We keep changing the furniture in Babylon and pretending it is home.
But Scripture says: “All we like sheep have gone astray; we have turned, every one, to his own way.” Isaiah 53:6

Our deepest problem is not that life has disappointed us.
It is that we have sinned against the God who made us.
We do not merely need encouragement. We need forgiveness.
We do not merely need a better plan. We need reconciliation.
we do not merely need directions. We need someone to bring us home.

The return from Babylon was not enough
God kept His promise.
After seventy years, the exiles returned.
Jerusalem was inhabited again. The temple was rebuilt.
The sacrifices resumed. 

But the deeper exile remained.
The people returned to the land, but sin was still in their hearts.
The walls could be rebuilt, but they could not keep death out.
The temple could be rebuilt, but animal sacrifices could not finally remove guilt.
The people had returned geographically, but humanity still needed to be brought home to God.
So God did something greater than sending another letter to the exiles.
He came to our address.
John 1:14 says: “And the Word became flesh and dwelt among us.”

The Son of God entered our world.He came into the far country.
He lived among exiles. Jesus lived the faithful life Israel had failed to live.
He loved the Father with all His heart. He obeyed every command.
He rejected every idol. He trusted the Father in every trial.
He was the faithful Son, the true Israel, the righteous King.

Yet He was treated as the exile.
Jesus was rejected by His people.

He was taken outside the city. He was nailed to a cross.
There He carried the judgment our rebellion deserves.
The cry from the cross was: “My God, my God, why have you forsaken me?”
Matt 27:46

Jesus entered the deepest exile.
He bore separation and judgment so that sinners could be brought near.
Paul writes: “But now in Christ Jesus you who once were far off have been brought near by the blood of Christ.” ~ Ephesians 2:13
At the cross, Jesus took the consequences of our sin.
The punishment fell on Him. The curse fell on Him.The judgment fell on Him.

Isaiah 53:6 continues: “And the LORD has laid on him the iniquity of us all.”
He was cast out so that we could be welcomed in.
He was treated as the stranger so that we could become children.
He took our exile so that we could come home.
