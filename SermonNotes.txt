The Final Messenger
Malachi 3–4
Focus: John the Baptist and the Coming Day

Introduction – The Last Words Before the Silence
We’ve reached the end of the Old Testament.
We’ve travelled a long way to get here. Creation. Fall. Flood. Abraham. Israel. Exodus. Kingdom. Exile. Return.
And all the way through, God has been making promises. A serpent-crushing Seed. Abraham’s offspring bring blessings to the nations. A greater Moses. David’s Son. The suffering Servant. The righteous Branch. A new heart and a new Spirit. The Son of Man is receiving an everlasting kingdom.
By the time we reach Malachi, Israel has returned from Babylon. The temple has been rebuilt. Sacrifices are happening again. On paper, it might look as though the exile is over.

But something is still badly wrong.

The people have returned to the land, but their hearts have not fully returned to God. The priests are offering polluted sacrifices. Worship has become casual. Marriage covenants are being broken. People are asking whether serving God is even worth it.
They have ‘come home geographically’, but the deeper problem that sent them into exile remains.
That is important. The Old Testament cannot end simply with Israel got back to Jerusalem.

The problem >> Babylon. problem = heart.

So Malachi ends the Old Testament looking forward.
A messenger is coming. The Lord is coming. And the Day is coming.
Then the voice of Old Testament prophecy falls silent. For centuries.
Persia rises and falls. Alexander the Great sweeps across the world. Greek culture spreads. Rome takes control. Generations are born and buried. 
Anitichus iV (Epiphanies) - abomination of desolation (salucid)
And still they wait.
It is like standing in the final hours before sunrise. The sky is still dark, but Malachi tells Israel that morning is coming.
That is the backbone of these chapters: The night is long, but the dawn is certain.

1. A Messenger Is Coming
Malachi 3:1
“Behold, I send my messenger, and he will prepare the way before me…”
The image is familiar in the ancient world. When a king travelled, messengers went ahead. Roads were prepared. Obstacles were removed. The population was warned.
The king is coming. Get ready. {parousia = the royal coming/arrival, occursus = the going out to meet/escort him in} 
Malachi says God will do something similar.
Before the Lord appears, a messenger will prepare His way.
Notice the wording: “I send my messenger, and he will prepare the way before me.”
The messenger is preparing the way for the LORD Himself.
That makes what happens at the beginning of the New Testament astonishing.
Mark opens his Gospel by reaching back to this promise and then introduces John the Baptist.
Jesus later quotes Malachi 3:1 and says of John:
“This is he of whom it is written, ‘Behold, I send my messenger before your face, who will prepare your way before you.’”
Matthew 11:10
John is not simply an eccentric preacher who happened to appear at the right moment.

He is the promised messenger.

After centuries of waiting, someone appears in the wilderness dressed in camel’s hair with a leather belt around his waist.
And his message is uncompromising: “Repent, for the kingdom of heaven is at hand.”

John is effectively shouting into the darkness: Morning is nearly here. The King is coming. Wake up.

~Preparing the Road~
Isaiah had spoken about preparing the way of the LORD:
“Every valley shall be lifted up, and every mountain and hill be made low.”

John takes that imagery and applies it spiritually.
The King doesn’t need Roman engineers to repair a road.
The road needing preparation is the human heart.
Pride must come down. Crookedness must be straightened. Sin must be confessed.

There is something uncomfortable about John because he refuses to congratulate religious people merely for being religious.
When Pharisees and Sadducees come to his baptism, he does not say, “Wonderful, the church leaders have arrived.”
He says: “Bear fruit in keeping with repentance.”

Religion itself can become another hiding place from God.

~Application~
We often want Jesus to enter our lives as a consultant.
Help with the marriage. Help with the anxiety. Help with the finances. Help me become a better version of myself.

John announces something far more disruptive: The King is coming.
Jesus doesn't come merely to improve our kingdom. He comes as King of His own.
Repentance means laying down our claim to the throne.

Martin Luther began the first of his Ninety-Five Theses with the claim that when Jesus said repent, He intended the whole Christian life to be one of repentance.

Not a prayer we once prayed. Not a box we once ticked.

A life continually turning from sin and turning toward Christ.
The first streak of dawn appears in Malachi: The messenger is coming.
And if the messenger is coming, the King cannot be far behind. 

2. The Lord Is Coming
Malachi 3:1–5
Malachi then moves immediately from the messenger to Someone far greater:“And the Lord whom you seek will suddenly come to his temple…”

That probably sounded wonderful. Israel had waited for God to act.
They wanted justice &restoration. They wanted God to deal with everything wrong in the world around them.

Then Malachi asks: “But who can endure the day of his coming, and who can stand when he appears?”

That changes the atmosphere completely. They were waiting for God to come and sort THEM out.
God says: What happens when I sort you out as well?

It is very easy to long for divine justice when we imagine ourselves standing comfortably on its right side.
We look at violence, corruption, exploitation, dishonesty and cruelty and say, “God needs to judge this.”
And He will.
But Malachi forces the question closer to home: Who can stand when He appears?
The problem with asking a perfectly holy God to expose evil is that His light does not stop at the sins we dislike.

It reaches us too.
Aleksandr Solzhenitsyn  observed that the dividing line between good and evil does not simply run between groups of people; it runs through every human heart.

Malachi understood that long before Solzhenitsyn.
The Lord comes: “like a refiner’s fire and like fullers’ soap.”

~The Refiner’s Fire~
A refiner heats precious metal until impurities rise to the surface and can be removed.
The purpose is not destruction of the metal. It is purification.
But purification is not comfortable.
The same is true of fuller’s soap. It is vigorous cleansing.
God is not interested in placing a thin religious coating over His people.
He intends to make them holy.
That explains why John the Baptist’s message is not merely:
The Messiah is coming!
He says: “He who is coming after me is mightier than I… He will baptize you with the Holy Spirit and fire.”

The language of Malachi is beginning to break into the New Testament.
The Lord has come to His temple.
And think about Jesus entering that temple.
He does not simply admire the architecture.
He overturns tables.
He drives out those turning worship into commerce.
He cleanses His Father’s house.

Malachi said the Lord would come to His temple as a purifier.
Then Jesus walks through the doors.

~Application~
There is a form of Christianity that wants Jesus as comfort but never as refiner.
We want Him to reassure us without exposing us.
But grace does not mean God looks at our sin and says it does not matter.
Grace means God loves sinners enough not to leave them untouched by His holiness.

The second streak of dawn appears:
The Lord is coming.
And Malachi asks us to consider whether we are ready to meet Him.

3. The Day Is Coming
Malachi 4:1–3
Then the horizon brightens further.
“For behold, the day is coming, burning like an oven…”
Malachi has moved from a messenger → the Lord, → o the Day of the LORD.
That Day has been developing throughout the prophets.
It is the moment when God finally acts.

Evil is judged. Justice is established. God’s enemies are defeated. His people are vindicated.
History is not an endless circle.
It is travelling toward an appointment.

C. S. Lewis used the striking picture of an author eventually walking onto the stage: when that happens, the play is over.
There is coming a moment when God will not merely send another warning into history.

He will bring history to its appointed conclusion.

For those who persist in rebellion, Malachi describes the Day as an oven.
But then the picture changes:
“But for you who fear my name, the sun of righteousness shall rise with healing in its wings.”

This is where our dawn image becomes explicit.

One sunrise. Two experiences.

The same sun that exposes everything hidden also brings warmth, light and healing.
For those who hate the light, the coming Day is terrifying.
For those who fear the Lord, it is the morning they have been waiting for.

The World Is Not Going to Stay Like This
That matters enormously.
Look honestly at the world and you can become cynical very quickly.
Wars continue. People exploit one another. Children suffer. Bodies fail. Governments disappoint. Churches fail. People die.
And sometimes wickedness appears to succeed.
Malachi’s answer is not to pretend that everything is fine.
His answer is: The morning is coming.
God has placed an expiry date on evil.
Cancer does not get the final word.
War does not get the final word.
Death does not get the final word.
Satan does not get the final word.
The grave does not get the final word.
Jesus does.
That is Christian hope.
Christian hope rests on the promise that history belongs to God and that Christ will return.

4. Remember While You Wait
Malachi 4:4–6
Then we reach the final few sentences of the Old Testament.
“Remember the law of my servant Moses…”
And then: “Behold, I will send you Elijah the prophet before the great and awesome day of the LORD comes.”
Notice who stands at the end.
Moses and Elijah.
The Law / The Prophets.
Remember what God has already spoken through Moses.
And look forward to the prophet who will announce what God is about to do.
Then the OT closes.
If you turned the page in a Christian Bible, the next thing you encounter is Matthew.
But historically there are centuries between those pages.
That blank space matters.
Waiting matters.
God has spoken, but the fulfilment has not yet appeared.

God Is Not Late: We often confuse silence with absence.
If God does not act on our timetable, we begin to wonder whether He is acting at all.
Israel waited centuries.
Then, at exactly the moment God intended, an angel appeared to an elderly priest called Zechariah.
His wife Elizabeth would have a son.
Malachi 4 is waking up.
The silence is ending.
John appears.
And Jesus says: “If you are willing to accept it, he is Elijah who is to come.”
Not Elijah reincarnated.
John comes carrying Elijah’s prophetic ministry — calling a compromised people back to God and announcing that the Lord is approaching.
The final messenger has arrived.
The horizon is glowing.
Malachi ends with the promise:
“Behold, I will send you Elijah the prophet before the great and awesome day of the LORD comes.”
Malachi 4:5
Then the Old Testament closes.



5. The Voice in the Wilderness
!! Then, suddenly, a voice is heard in the Judean wilderness.
“In those days John the Baptist came preaching in the wilderness of Judea, ‘Repent, for the kingdom of heaven is at hand.’”
Matthew 3:1–2
John does not arrive in Jerusalem dressed like a priest. He does not appear in the palace or build a platform in the temple courts.
He appears in the wilderness.
Camel’s hair. Leather belt. Living simply. Calling Israel to repentance.
Everything about him carries the echo of Elijah.
2 Kings describes Elijah as wearing a garment of hair with a leather belt around his waist. Now, centuries later, another prophet appears dressed the same way and preaching with the same uncomfortable directness.
This is not accidental.
Before John is even born, the angel tells his father Zechariah:
“He will go before him in the spirit and power of Elijah… to make ready for the Lord a people prepared.”
Luke 1:17
Malachi’s promise is being fulfilled.
The silence is ending.
The final messenger has arrived.
A Voice, Not the Word
When people begin asking John who he is, he refuses to make himself the centre.
He says: “I am the voice of one crying out in the wilderness, ‘Make straight the way of the Lord.’” John 1:23
That is a remarkable description of himself.

A voice.

A voice exists to carry a message. Once the message has been heard, attention should move beyond the voice to what is being said.
John knows his role.
He is not building a movement around himself.
He is not gathering followers so that people will remember the name John the Baptist.
He is preparing people for Someone else.
That itself is a challenge to the instinct we saw all the way back at Babel.
Babel said: “Let us make a name for ourselves.”
John effectively says: Forget my name. The King is coming.

Prepare the Way
And John’s message is very simple: “Repent.”
That is how the road is prepared.
The problem is not that Rome has built the wrong roads.
Pride must come down. Crookedness must be straightened. Sin must be confessed.
John even confronts the religious leaders:
“Bear fruit in keeping with repentance.”
Matthew 3:8
Being descended from Abraham is not enough.
Standing near the temple is not enough.
Knowing the Scriptures is not enough.
The Lord is coming.
The question is whether the people are ready to meet Him.

John Chrysostom described John as a kind of herald running before the King, calling everyone to prepare themselves for His arrival.

That is exactly what John is doing. The long night is nearly over.
The horizon is beginning to brighten.
A voice is crying in the wilderness.
Prepare the way of the Lord.
But John knows something else.
He may be the messenger Malachi promised.
He may be the voice Isaiah anticipated.
But he is not the One Israel has been waiting for.
Someone greater is already coming behind him.

6. Behold the Lamb
John’s whole ministry points beyond himself.
Crowds are coming to him. People are listening. Some are beginning to wonder whether John himself might be the Christ.
Luke tells us:
“The people were in expectation, and all were questioning in their hearts concerning John, whether he might be the Christ.”
Luke 3:15
John could have enjoyed the attention.
Instead, he keeps redirecting it.
“I baptize you with water, but he who is mightier than I is coming, the strap of whose sandals I am not worthy to untie.”
Luke 3:16
John knows exactly who he is.
And he knows who he is not.
He == messenger. == voice.
He ¬= King.
John 1 makes it explicit:
“He was not the light, but came to bear witness about the light.”
That is the point. John is not the sunrise.
He is the voice in the darkness telling people that the sun is about to rise.

Then one day Jesus approaches.
And John says:
“Behold, the Lamb of God, who takes away the sin of the world!”
John 1:29
That sentence gathers up so much of the Old Testament.
A lamb in Egypt, whose blood marked out a people for deliverance.
A lamb offered in sacrifice.
Isaiah’s Servant: “Like a lamb that is led to the slaughter…”
And now John looks at Jesus and says:
There He is. ¬ simply a teacher /  prophet / reformer calling Israel back to God.
= The Lamb of God.

The Messenger Must Decrease
Later, when John’s disciples become concerned that people are beginning to follow Jesus instead of him, John answers:
“He must increase, but I must decrease.”
John 3:30

That may be one of the clearest summaries of faithful ministry anywhere in Scripture.
John has spent his life preparing people for this moment.
So when Christ arrives, John does not compete with Him.
He steps aside.
That is very different from the instinct we saw at Babel: “Let us make a name for ourselves.”
John’s ministry says: His name matters. Not mine.

That is also a useful test of Christian ministry.
Does it make people impressed with the messenger?
Or does it make them look past the messenger to Christ?
Paul later says: “What then is Apollos? What is Paul? Servants through whom you believed…”

The messenger matters.
But only because of the One being announced.

The Lamb Who Takes Away Sin
John does not merely say: Behold the King.
He says:  Behold the Lamb.
That matters because Malachi has already asked: “Who can endure the day of his coming, and who can stand when he appears?”

If the Lord is coming in holiness, if He comes like refining fire, then how can sinners stand?

John’s answer begins here.
The King comes as a Lamb.
Before He comes finally to judge, He comes first to save.
Before the Day of judgment falls upon the world, the Lamb will bear judgment Himself.
John sees Jesus and identifies the One who has come to deal with the deepest problem Malachi exposed.
Not Rome. Selurians, Greeks, Not Babylon Asyrians
Not merely corruption in the priesthood.

Sin.
“Who takes away the sin of the world.”
This is why John’s ministry cannot end with John.
Repentance prepares the road. But repentance is not the Saviour.
Baptism is a sign. But baptism is not the Saviour.
John is the messenger. But John is not the Saviour.
Everything points to Christ.

~Application~
There is always a temptation to stop one step short of Jesus.
We can admire preaching.
Admire theology. Admire a church. Admire a particular Christian leader.
Even admire repentance itself. But none of those can carry our sin.
John will not let us stop with him.
He points away from himself and says:
Look there.
Behold the Lamb.
And now the question Malachi asked becomes unavoidable:
If the Lord is coming, and the Lamb has appeared, who can stand when He comes?
That takes us directly to the cross.

7. Who Can Stand?
Now we can return to Malachi’s question:
“Who can endure the day of his coming, and who can stand when he appears?”
If God is perfectly holy and we are sinners, who can stand?
Malachi has already exposed religious people whose worship was corrupt.
John’s answer is astonishing: “Behold, the Lamb of God.”
The Lord comes to judge sin.
But before the final judgment falls upon the world, the Judge steps into the place of the guilty.
The imagery of Malachi takes us toward the cross.
The Day of the LORD is associated with darkness.
And when Jesus is crucified: “From the sixth hour there was darkness over all the land…”
Day turns to darkness.
The innocent One stands where guilty people should stand.
Isaiah had said: “The LORD has laid on him the iniquity of us all.”
At Calvary, Christ bears the judgment His people deserve.
The fire of divine judgment falls upon Him.
The Lamb is sacrificed.
The King wears a crown of thorns.
The Lord who came to His temple is taken outside the city and crucified.
And then something wonderful happens.
After the darkness—
morning.
The women go to the tomb at dawn.
The stone is rolled away.
The grave is empty.
The rising of Jesus Christ is the guarantee that the final sunrise is coming.

Conclusion — The Night Is Nearly Over
Malachi stands at the end of the Old Testament and says:

A messenger is coming. The Lord is coming. The Day is coming.

Then the Scriptures fall silent.
Centuries later a voice cries in the wilderness:
Prepare the way of the Lord.
The messenger has arrived.
Then John points: Behold the Lamb.
The Lord has arrived.
He lived. He died. He rose.
But Malachi’s Day still points beyond Christ’s first coming.
The New Testament teaches that the Jesus who came once in humility will come again in glory.
That means we live between dawn and full daylight.
The decisive light has already broken into the world.
The tomb is empty.
Christ is reigning.
The Spirit has been poured out.
But we still wait for the morning when every shadow disappears.
Paul puts it beautifully in Romans 13: “The night is far gone; the day is at hand.”
That is where the church lives.
The night is far gone. The Day is at hand.
So do not mistake God’s patience for God’s absence.
Do not look at darkness and assume darkness is winning.
Do not make peace with sin because judgment appears delayed.
Do not build your life as though this world will carry on forever.
The sun is already appearing over the horizon.
And Malachi leaves us with a question that the Gospel finally answers:
Who can stand when He appears?
By ourselves, we can’t
We stand because the One who will return as Judge first came as the Lamb.
Christ stood in the place of sinners. Christ bore the judgment.
Christ rose from the grave.
And everyone who turns from sin and trusts in Him can look toward the coming Day not merely with terror - but with hope.
Malachi looked into the darkness and promised morning.
John stood at first light and pointed toward the rising Sun.
And the church now looks toward the horizon and says:
The night is far gone. The Day is at hand.
The King is coming.

Prayer
Confess where I have grown comfortable with sin or casual about Christ’s return. Thank You, Lord, for keeping every promise through the long years of waiting. I praise You for sending John to prepare the way, and Jesus as the Lamb who takes away sin. Thank You, Jesus, for bearing judgment at the cross and rising from the grave. Holy Spirit, produce in me genuine repentance rather than outward religion. Make me faithful while I wait, and give me confidence that the night will not last forever. I pray too for those who are not ready to meet the King—draw them to Christ in repentance and faith.
