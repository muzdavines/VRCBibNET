The Suffering Servant
Text: Isaiah 52:13–53:12
Focus: Christ crucified and victorious

Introduction: Believe the Report
Isaiah 53 is sometimes called “the forbidden chapter.”
That title needs care. It has not been removed from the Hebrew Bible, and Jewish people are not forbidden from reading it. But Isaiah 52:13–53:12 is not included in the regular synagogue cycle of readings from the Prophets.
Whatever we make of that nickname, this passage cannot be avoided.
Isaiah asks: “Who has believed what he has heard from us?” vv 53:1

Who is this Servant?
He is rejected, although innocent.
He is pierced for the sins of others.
He is led silently to death.
He is buried with a rich man.

Yet afterwards He lives, sees His offspring and is exalted.

In Acts 8, when the Ethiopian official asks who Isaiah is describing, Philip begins with this passage and tells him “the good news about Jesus.”

Isaiah brings us directly to Christ.

Where We Are in the Arc
Last time, in Jeremiah 29, God’s people were living in exile.
God promised to bring them back from Babylon. But returning to Jerusalem couldn’t solve their deepest problem.
The walls could be rebuilt, but they could not keep death out.
The temple could be restored, but repeated animal sacrifices could not finally remove sin.
They needed more than a road home from Babylon.
They needed a way home to God. Isaiah reveals how God would provide it.
He would send His Servant.
And the Servant would bring His people home by taking their place.
~The Victory Report~
Imagine a nation waiting for news from the battlefield.
At last, the king’s official report arrives.

It begins by announcing the outcome:
•    The Servant has succeeded.
•    He will be exalted.
•    The victory has been won.

Then it describes a Servant who is disfigured, rejected, wounded, killed and buried.
Everything appears to say defeat.
But the report explains that His wounds were not evidence of failure.
He was carrying our sins.
His death was how the victory was won.
Then the report ends where it began.
The Servant lives.
He justifies many.
He divides the spoil.
•    It looked like defeat, but the report was not finished.


{Read Isaiah 52:13–53:3}


1. The Victorious Servant Is Rejected
Isa 52:13–53:3 “BEHOLD, my servant shall act wisely; he shall be high and lifted up, and shall be exalted.”

Before Isaiah describes any suffering, God announces the result.

The Servant will succeed.

The cross will not be an accident.
Judas will betray Jesus.
The leaders will condemn Him.
Pilate will hand Him over.
The soldiers will crucify Him.
But none of them will overturn the purpose of God.

The world will say, “He is finished.”
Jesus will say: “It is finished.” John 19:30

Those are not the same statement.
One means that His enemies have defeated Him.
The other means that His saving work has been completed.
But first the Servant will be brought terribly low:  “His appearance was so marred, beyond human semblance.”
He will be beaten, mocked, scourged and crucified.
Yet through that suffering: “He shall sprinkle many nations.”
The language is priestly. His blood will bring cleansing, not merely to Israel, but to people from many nations.
Isaiah then asks: “Who has believed what he has heard from us?”
The saving power of God appears in an unexpected form: “Like a young plant,
and like a root out of dry ground.”
No palace. No earthly crown. No army.
No outward majesty that compelled people to follow Him.

Instead: “He was despised and rejected by men, a man of sorrows and acquainted with grief.”

The Father says: “Behold my servant.”
Humanity says: “We do not want Him.”

The cross offends our pride because it tells us we could not save ourselves.

Our morality could not remove guilt.
Our wisdom could not find a road back to God.
Our strength could not defeat death.
God had to come for us.

Application
We are still attracted to impressive saviours.
Platforms. Personalities. Political strength. Visible success.

But God’s greatest saving work came through apparent weakness.
We must never become ashamed of Christ crucified.
It looked like defeat, but the report was not finished.

{Read Isaiah 53:4–6}


2. The Servant Was Wounded for Us
Isaiah 53:4–6 These verses are the centre of the passage.
Notice the repeated contrast:
He and we.
His and our. “Surely he has borne our griefs
and carried our sorrows.”

Those watching thought He was suffering for His own wrongdoing: “We esteemed him stricken, smitten by God, and afflicted.”
They were right that judgment was falling upon Him.
They were wrong about why.
•    “But he was pierced for our transgressions; he was crushed for our iniquities.”

Not His transgressions. Ours!
Not His iniquities. Ours!
He suffered FOR not BESIDE sinners.
He did not merely experience human pain, bore human guilt.
“Upon him was the chastisement that brought us peace.”

Peace here is not merely an inward feeling. It is reconciliation with God.
We had become His enemies through sin. The Servant received the judgment so that we could receive peace. 

“And with his wounds we are healed.”

The immediate problem in the passage is transgression and iniquity.
Isaiah is not promising that every believer will immediately be healed from every illness.
He is announcing the deeper healing we all need.
Sin forgiven. Guilt removed. Enemies reconciled to God.
Verse 6 describes us: “All we like sheep have gone astray;
we have turned, every one, to his own way.”
We do not all wander in the same direction.
Some wander through obvious immorality,  pride,  pleasure, bitterness.
Some through respectable religion.
But beneath it all is the same declaration:
My way.
John Stott wrote: “The essence of sin is man substituting himself for God, while the essence of salvation is God substituting himself for man.”
We put ourselves where God deserves to be.
At the cross, God the Son willingly stands where guilty humanity deserves to be.
“And the LORD has laid on him
the iniquity of us all.”
God does not pretend sin does not matter.
Sin is judged.
But the judgment falls upon the Servant.
He bears the guilt.
He answers the debt.
He purchases peace.
His wounds are not evidence that salvation failed.
His wounds are how salvation was accomplished.
It looked like defeat, but this was how the victory was won.

Application
You cannot justify yourself by comparing yourself with someone worse.
Isaiah includes us all:
“All we like sheep have gone astray.”
You do not need to make yourself appear less guilty.
You need a Substitute.
And if you belong to Christ, your assurance rests here.
The Servant has borne your judgment.

{Read Isaiah 53:7–10}

3. The Innocent Servant Gives Himself
Isaiah 53:7–10 - “He was oppressed, and he was afflicted,
yet he opened not his mouth.”

The Servant is not silent because the accusations are true.
He is silent because He has willingly come to give His life.
.           “Like a lamb that is led to the slaughter.”

Jesus stands before false witnesses, religious leaders and Pilate.
He does not mount a defence designed to escape the cross.

He had already said: “I lay down my life for the sheep.” John 10:15
And:“No one takes it from me, but I lay it down of my own accord.”
John 10:18
Jesus is not an unwilling victim.
The Father sends.
The Son willingly comes.

Verse 8 says: “By oppression and judgment he was taken away.”
Human justice fails Him.
Witnesses lie.
Leaders manipulate.
Pilate declares Him innocent, then hands Him over to die.
The true Judge is condemned by unjust judges.

Yet Isaiah again explains why: “Stricken for the transgression of my people.”
Verse 9 emphasises His innocence: “He had done no violence, and there was no deceit in his mouth.”

Only an innocent sacrifice could bear the guilt of others.
Jesus is crucified among criminals, but buried in the tomb of Joseph of Arimathea, a rich man.
Isaiah had said: “They made his grave with the wicked and with a rich man in his death.”
Then verse 10 takes us behind the actions of Judas, Pilate and the leaders: “Yet it was the will of the LORD to crush him.”

This does not mean the Father delighted in cruelty towards an unwilling Son.
Father, Son and Spirit are united in redemption.
Human beings act wickedly.
God acts savingly through their wicked actions.
The cross is not an accident.
The Servant makes: “An offering for guilt.”
The sacrifices of Israel had taught that guilt required atonement.
But animals could never finally remove human sin.

Then Christ came. Truly human, able to represent us.
Completely righteous, with no guilt of His own.
Willingly offering Himself in our place.
Jesus is both the Servant and the sacrifice.

Application
The enemies of Jesus said: “He saved others; he cannot save himself.”
But because He would not save Himself, He could save others.

4. The Servant Lives and Divides the Spoil
Isaiah 53:10–12
The Servant has died and been buried.
Then Isaiah says: “He shall see his offspring; he shall prolong his days.”
How can someone who has been cut off from the living prolong His days?
How can the dead Servant see His offspring?
The report points us beyond the grave.
The Servant lives again.
His offspring are the redeemed people brought into God’s family through His sacrifice.
“Out of the anguish of his soul he shall see and be satisfied.”
He will see sinners forgiven. Rebels reconciled.
Wanderers brought home. The guilty made righteous. And He will be satisfied.
The resurrection declares that the sacrifice has been accepted.
Sin has been answered. Death has been defeated.
The Servant has been vindicated.
.       It looked like defeat, but the battlefield could not keep Him.

His victory achieves: “My servant shall make many to be accounted righteous,
and he shall bear their iniquities.”

The Servant is righteous. We are guilty.
Yet because He bears our iniquities, those who trust Him are accounted righteous.
God does not ignore their sin. Their sin has been judged in Christ.
Our guilt is counted to Him. His righteousness is counted to us.
Then the report returns to battlefield language: “He shall divide the spoil with the strong.”

The Servant returns as the victorious King.

Thomas Watson said: “Christ died both as a purchaser and as a conqueror.”
Servant ¬ empty-handed. He brings a redeemed people with Him.

We are not trophies of our goodness. We are trophies of His grace.

Isaiah closes by summarising His work: “He poured out his soul to death
and was numbered with the transgressors; yet he bore the sin of many,
and makes intercession for the transgressor.”

He died among sinners. He died for sinners.
He rose to represent and intercede for sinners.
The Suffering Servant is the victorious Servant.

Conclusion: Believe the Report
Isaiah began: “Who has believed what he has heard from us?”
The report has been opened.  We turned to our own way.
•    He was pierced for our transgressions.
•    He was crushed for our iniquities.
•    He received the chastisement that brought us peace.
•    He was silent before His accusers.
•    He died.
•    He was buried.
But the report was not finished.
He lives. He justifies many. He divides the spoil.
