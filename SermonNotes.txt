The Cross and the Curse
Bible Arc Sermon Series 2026 | VR Fellowship
Scripture: Galatians 3:10–14; Mark 15
Focus: Christ bore our curse
Central truth: The innocent King bore the judgment of guilty sinners so that the blessing promised to Abraham might come to the nations.
Introduction: From the Donkey to the Cross
Last time, we stood on the road leading into Jerusalem. We watched Jesus enter the city riding on a donkey, fulfilling the prophecy of Zechariah 9:9. Not a king arriving on a warhorse surrounded by soldiers, but the promised King coming in humility. The crowds spread their cloaks and cried out, "Hosanna! Blessed is he who comes in the name of the Lord!"

But there was something they didn't fully understand. They were expecting a king who would bring deliverance. Many were looking for freedom from Roman occupation and the restoration of Israel. They wanted salvation, but they didn't yet understand the kind of salvation they really needed.

And now, as we move into Mark 15, the scene changes dramatically. Between the triumphal entry and the trial before Pilate, Jesus has shared the Passover with His disciples, been betrayed by Judas, denied by Peter, and prayed in Gethsemane. In that garden, knowing what lay ahead, He prayed, "Yet not what I will, but what you will" (Mark 14:36).

That prayer matters. The cross wasn't something Jesus stumbled into unexpectedly. He knew what was coming, He knew the cost, and He willingly submitted to the Father's purpose. So when we find Him standing before Pilate, we must remember that this is the King who has deliberately chosen the road to Calvary.

The King who entered Jerusalem to cries of "Hosanna" is now standing before a Roman governor. The crowd gathered before Pilate is crying, "Crucify him!" The One welcomed into Jerusalem will be led outside the city to die. The One recognised as King will be crowned with thorns. And the One who came bringing blessing will bear a curse.

From a human perspective, it looks like complete defeat. But today I want us to consider the cross through two passages of Scripture. Mark 15 tells us what happened. Galatians 3 tells us what it meant.

There's one word at the centre of Paul's explanation: curse. We use that word rather loosely. Someone has a run of bad luck and says they're cursed. In some Christian circles, almost every illness or financial difficulty is attributed to some hidden curse that needs to be broken. But that's not what Paul is talking about. He's speaking about the righteous judgement of a holy God against sin. And he makes an astonishing claim: Jesus Christ bore that curse on behalf of sinners.

Now, Paul was writing to churches where Gentile believers were being told that faith in Jesus wasn't enough. They needed to take on the obligations of the Mosaic law, particularly circumcision, to be fully accepted as God's people. Paul recognises what's at stake. If we must establish our righteousness through the law, then Christ is not sufficient. And if Christ is not sufficient, we've lost the gospel.

Read Galatians 3:10–14.



1. The Problem: We Cannot Save Ourselves
Galatians 3:10–12
Paul begins with a statement that should make us uncomfortable. Verse 10 says, "For all who rely on works of the law are under a curse." Then he quotes Deuteronomy 27:26: "Cursed be everyone who does not abide by all things written in the Book of the Law, and do them."

Notice that word, all. Not most, not the important commandments, not the ones we find convenient. All of them. And they must be obeyed, not merely understood or admired.

Now, Paul isn't saying God's law is bad. Romans 7:12 tells us the law is holy, righteous and good. The problem isn't the law. The problem is us.

Think about the warning lights on your car's dashboard. You're driving along and the engine management light appears. You might find it irritating. You might wish it would disappear. You might even be tempted to cover it with a piece of tape. But covering the warning light doesn't repair the engine. It's telling you that something is wrong.

And in a similar way, God's law exposes the condition of the human heart. It reveals our sin, but it cannot, by itself, repair what sin has damaged. You could memorise every commandment in Scripture and still be a sinner.

Let's return briefly to our Bible Arc. In Genesis 3, Adam and Eve rebelled against God. Sin entered creation, and with it came judgement. The serpent was cursed, the ground was cursed because of Adam, and human life became marked by suffering, frustration and death. As the story continued, humanity's rebellion spread. Cain killed Abel, wickedness increased before the flood, and at Babel humanity tried to make a name for itself rather than honour God.

Then God called Abraham and promised that through him all the families of the earth would be blessed. Eventually, Abraham's descendants became Israel. God delivered them from Egypt, brought them to Sinai and gave them His law. And what happened? Even while Moses was receiving God's commandments, the people were making a golden calf!

We watched that pattern repeat throughout Israel's history. Disobedience under the judges, unfaithfulness under the kings, and eventually exile. God had warned Israel that disobedience under His covenant carried consequences.

Now, the curse upon the ground in Genesis 3 and the covenant curses in Deuteronomy aren't exactly the same thing. Israel's covenant obligations weren't simply imposed upon every Gentile nation. But these passages belong to the same tragic story of human rebellion. Israel's history demonstrates that even a nation given God's law could not rescue itself from sin. And the problem goes beyond Israel. Romans 3:23 tells us, "For all have sinned and fall short of the glory of God."

That's every one of us.

Think about your own life for a moment. Suppose God examined everything you've ever done. Not simply your public behaviour, not the version of yourself you present to friends and family, and since we're meeting in VR, not simply the avatar or identity you choose to present to others. Everything. Every thought, every word, every action, every motive.

How would you stand? Have you always loved God with all your heart? Have you always loved your neighbour as yourself? Not occasionally, not when it was convenient, but always? None of us can honestly answer yes.

Martin Luther understood this. In his Heidelberg Disputation of 1518, he wrote: "The law says, 'do this,' and it is never done. Grace says, 'believe in this,' and everything is already done."

Luther wasn't suggesting that Christians shouldn't obey God. He was distinguishing between trying to earn righteousness and receiving it through faith in Christ. The law demands; grace gives. The law exposes our failure; Christ provides what we lack.

Paul makes this distinction in verses 11 and 12. First, quoting Habakkuk 2:4, he says, "The righteous shall live by faith." Then, quoting Leviticus 18:5, he says, "The one who does them shall live by them."

Do you see the contrast? If you're relying upon your obedience to establish your righteousness, then obedience is what you must produce. And the law doesn't offer a reduced standard because we tried our best.

Paul isn't teaching that faith and obedience are enemies. Genuine faith produces obedience. He's asking what we're relying upon for our acceptance before God. Our performance, or Christ?

Because the answer to our sin isn't simply to try harder. The answer is Christ, the One who obeyed where we disobeyed, and the One who bore the curse our disobedience deserved.

2. The Innocent King and the Guilty Prisoner
Read Mark 15:1–15.

Mark begins with Jesus standing before Pontius Pilate. The religious leaders have handed Him over to the Roman authorities. Pilate asks Him, "Are you the King of the Jews?" And Jesus replies, "You have said so."

Think about the contrast. Only days earlier, Jesus entered Jerusalem as its promised King. Now He stands before the Roman governor, being questioned about His kingship. Pilate is looking for a political threat. Is Jesus planning an uprising? Is He challenging Caesar? But Jesus hasn't gathered an army or organised a rebellion.

And Pilate knows something is wrong. Mark 15:10 tells us that Pilate recognised the chief priests had handed Jesus over out of envy.

Then Mark introduces another man: Barabbas. Verse 7 tells us that Barabbas was imprisoned among rebels who had committed murder during an insurrection. He wasn't simply someone with unpopular political opinions. He was associated with violent rebellion.

And suddenly Mark presents two very different prisoners. Barabbas, associated with rebellion and bloodshed, and Jesus, the One who had healed the sick, welcomed sinners and preached the kingdom of God. One guilty, the other innocent.

Pilate offers the crowd a choice. Which man should be released? Barabbas, or Jesus, the King of the Jews? The crowd demands Barabbas. Then Pilate asks what he should do with Jesus. "Crucify him!"

Verse 15 tells us that Pilate, wishing to satisfy the crowd, released Barabbas and handed Jesus over to be crucified.

Now picture that scene. Barabbas walks free. The guilty prisoner is released, and Jesus, the innocent King, is led away to die.

We don't know what happened to Barabbas afterwards. Mark doesn't tell us, and we shouldn't invent his story. But there's a remarkable picture here. The guilty man is set free while the innocent man goes to his death.

And that brings us to one of the central truths of the gospel: substitution.

We sometimes hear people say that Jesus died to demonstrate God's love. And that's true. But if that's all we say, we've missed something essential. Jesus didn't simply die to provide an inspiring example of sacrificial love. He died to accomplish something. He died for sinners.

John Stott, in The Cross of Christ, expressed it memorably: "The essence of sin is man substituting himself for God, while the essence of salvation is God substituting himself for man."

Think about that. Sin is humanity trying to take God's place. We want to decide what's right and wrong, establish our own standards and live independently of our Creator. But in salvation, God does something extraordinary. In the person of His Son, He takes the place of sinners.

Isaiah 53:6 says, "The LORD has laid on him the iniquity of us all." Our sins, His suffering. Our guilt, His judgement.

Now, someone might ask whether it's just for an innocent person to suffer for someone else's wrongdoing. That's a fair question. If an earthly judge took an innocent stranger and punished him in place of a criminal, we'd rightly call that injustice. But that's not what's happening at Calvary.

The cross isn't God finding an innocent outsider to punish. It is God Himself, in the person of His Son, graciously providing the sacrifice our salvation requires. The Father sends the Son, and the Son willingly gives Himself.

Jesus said in John 10:18, "No one takes it from me, but I lay it down of my own accord." And in Mark 10:45, He explains that He came to give His life as a ransom for many.

But why couldn't God simply overlook sin? Why did there have to be a cross?

Imagine a judge hearing a serious criminal case. The evidence is overwhelming, the defendant is guilty, and the victims have suffered terribly. Then the judge announces, "Well, I'm a loving person, so I'm going to ignore everything that's happened."

Would we call that justice? Of course not. We'd be outraged, and rightly so. Wrongdoing matters. Victims matter. Justice matters. Yet when it comes to our own sin before God, we're often tempted to ask Him to do exactly that. Just overlook it. Just forget about it.

But God doesn't pretend evil is harmless. He is perfectly holy and perfectly just. And the extraordinary message of the gospel is that God Himself provides the sacrifice through which sinners can be forgiven without His justice being abandoned.

Romans 3:26 tells us that God is both just and the justifier of the one who has faith in Jesus. Justice isn't abandoned and mercy isn't denied. They meet at the cross.

And now Paul explains precisely what Christ's death accomplished.

3. Christ Became a Curse for Us
Galatians 3:13; Mark 15:16–41

Listen again to Galatians 3:13: "Christ redeemed us from the curse of the law by becoming a curse for us."

Those words ought to stop us in our tracks. Paul doesn't merely say that Jesus suffered or died a shameful death. He says Christ became a curse for us.

Then he quotes Deuteronomy 21:23: "Cursed is everyone who is hanged on a tree." Under the law of Moses, the public hanging of an executed person's body was associated with disgrace and God's judgement.

Now, Deuteronomy wasn't describing Roman crucifixion, which belonged to a later historical setting. But Paul recognises the theological significance. The sinless Son of God has been publicly displayed upon a wooden cross. He has been placed in the position associated with the cursed.

But Jesus hadn't sinned. He hadn't broken God's law or rebelled against the Father. So why was He hanging there?

Paul gives the answer: For us. Not for His own sins, but for ours.


The King crowned with thorns
Read Mark 15:16–32.
Mark tells us that the Roman soldiers dress Jesus in a purple cloak. They twist together a crown of thorns and place it on His head. They mock Him, saying, "Hail, King of the Jews!" They strike Him, spit on Him and kneel before Him in pretend worship.

Everything is designed to humiliate. To them, Jesus is a joke. Here is a Jewish prisoner claiming to be a king, apparently helpless before Roman authority.

But there's terrible irony in the scene. They call Him King in mockery, yet He really is the King. They bow before Him in contempt, yet one day every knee will bow before Him.

And consider that crown. A crown of thorns.

We've seen thorns before in our Bible Arc. Genesis 3. After Adam's rebellion, the ground was cursed, and thorns and thistles became part of humanity's painful struggle.

Now, Mark doesn't explicitly connect the crown of thorns to Genesis 3. The soldiers certainly weren't trying to teach biblical theology! They were mocking Jesus. But when we read Scripture as one unfolding story, the imagery is striking.

The King who has come to deal with the curse of sin is crowned with one of the signs of the fallen creation.

And then they lead Him away to crucify Him.

Mark's description is remarkably restrained. Verse 24 simply says, "And they crucified him." Four words. He doesn't give us a lengthy account of the physical suffering. He directs our attention towards who is dying, and why.

Above Jesus' head is the inscription, "The King of the Jews." Rome intends it as an accusation, but it announces the truth. Jesus is the King.

Then the religious leaders begin mocking Him. Verse 31 records their words: "He saved others; he cannot save himself."

They mean that as an insult, but consider what they're saying. "He saved others." They know something of His ministry, yet they cannot understand why the One who saved others refuses to save Himself.

And here's the irony. His refusal to save Himself is precisely how He is saving others.

Jesus wasn't trapped by Roman soldiers. He hadn't lost His authority. He was willingly giving His life. He could have avoided the cross, but then the work He came to accomplish would remain undone. So He stayed, not because He lacked the power to escape, but because He had come to give His life as a ransom.

*Christ redeemed us*

Return to Paul's words: "Christ redeemed us."

The Greek word translated "redeemed" is exēgorasen, from exagorazō. It comes from language associated with purchasing or buying out, and here describes Christ securing our release.

Think of someone carrying a debt they could never repay. Every attempt to settle the account falls short. Then someone else pays it in full. Not reduced, not rearranged into more manageable instalments, but paid.

Of course, sin is far more serious than a financial debt. It's personal rebellion against our Creator. But the illustration helps us understand the completeness of Christ's work.

Jesus hasn't offered to pay most of the cost and leave us to find the remainder. He has accomplished redemption.

And we must be clear about something. When Paul says Christ became a curse, he doesn't mean Jesus became personally sinful. He remained perfectly righteous. Second Corinthians 5:21 tells us that God made Him who knew no sin to be sin for us, so that in Him we might become the righteousness of God.

The innocent Christ bore the judgement of the guilty.

→ The darkness and the cry

Read Mark 15:33–39.
Verse 33 tells us that darkness covered the land from the sixth hour until the ninth hour, approximately midday until three in the afternoon.

Throughout Scripture, darkness can accompany divine judgement. In Exodus 10, darkness descended upon Egypt during God's judgements. And in Amos 8:9, God declares, "I will make the sun go down at noon and darken the earth in broad daylight."

That's a remarkable parallel. Darkness at noon. Judgement in the middle of the day. And here, at the cross, as Jesus hangs dying, darkness descends.

Then comes verse 34. Jesus cries out, "Eloi, Eloi, lema sabachthani?" And Mark translates it: "My God, my God, why have you forsaken me?"

These words come from Psalm 22, and they're among the most difficult and profound words Jesus ever spoke.

What does He mean? Has the Father abandoned the Son? Has the Trinity somehow been broken?

No. We must never suggest that the eternal relationship between Father, Son and Holy Spirit was destroyed at Calvary. The Trinity did not cease to exist. The Father didn't stop being the Father, and the Son didn't stop being God.

But neither should we minimise the suffering expressed in this cry. Jesus isn't simply quoting a psalm without experiencing the anguish it describes. He is bearing the judgement of sin, and that judgement is real.

The eternal unity of the Trinity remains, yet the incarnate Son genuinely suffers as the sin-bearing substitute. Exactly how that suffering was experienced is deeper than we can fully explain. But Scripture tells us its purpose.

Isaiah 53 says the Lord laid upon Him the iniquity of us all. Galatians 3 says Christ became a curse for us. And 1 Peter 2:24 says He bore our sins in His body on the tree.

Now, I don't want to minimise the physical suffering of crucifixion. It was horrific. But thousands of people were crucified by the Romans. What makes the cross of Christ unique isn't simply the intensity of His physical pain. It's who He is and what He is accomplishing.

This is the eternal Son of God, made flesh. The One who had never sinned, the One who had always delighted to do the Father's will, now bearing the condemnation that sinners deserve.

The cross tells us that sin is far more serious than we usually imagine. We live in a culture that often treats wrongdoing as little more than poor judgement. People make mistakes. Nobody's perfect. Everybody has their faults.

But Scripture speaks about sin as rebellion against the holy God who made us. And the cost of our redemption was nothing less than the death of God's beloved Son.

That ought to change the way we think about sin. But it should also change the way we think about God's love, because the cross demonstrates how far God has gone to redeem sinners.

And there's something else we must notice. Jesus is quoting Psalm 22. Although that psalm begins with the cry of abandonment, it doesn't end there. It moves through suffering towards vindication and praise. By the end, the nations are called to worship the Lord.

Psalm 22:27 says, "All the ends of the earth shall remember and turn to the LORD."

Isn't that extraordinary? A psalm beginning with the anguish of the suffering righteous one ends with the nations turning to God. And that's precisely where Paul's argument in Galatians is taking us. Christ bears the curse, and the nations receive the blessing.

Then Mark records the death itself. Verse 37: "And Jesus uttered a loud cry and breathed his last."

The King is dead.

→The curtain was torn

Immediately after Jesus dies, Mark tells us something extraordinary. Verse 38: "And the curtain of the temple was torn in two, from top to bottom."

The temple was the centre of Israel's worship, the place associated with God's special presence among His covenant people. Under the old covenant, access to the Most Holy Place was restricted. Only the high priest could enter, once a year on the Day of Atonement, with sacrificial blood.

That arrangement taught Israel something essential. God is holy, human beings are sinful, and sinners cannot approach a holy God on their own terms.

But now Jesus has died, and the curtain is torn. Mark doesn't specify precisely which temple curtain was torn, but he tells us when it happened and that it was torn from top to bottom. The direction points to God's action.

Hebrews 10:19–22 helps us understand the theological significance. Because of Jesus' blood, believers now have confidence to enter God's presence.

Not because God has become less holy, or because sin has become less serious, but because Christ has accomplished what the old sacrifices could only anticipate.

He is the true High Priest, the perfect sacrifice, the righteous One who bore the judgement of sinners. And through Him, we are brought near to God.

Then Mark records another remarkable moment. A Roman centurion stands facing Jesus and declares, "Truly this man was the Son of God!"

Remember how Mark began his Gospel: "The beginning of the gospel of Jesus Christ, the Son of God." At the beginning, Mark tells us who Jesus is. And now, standing at the foot of the cross, a Roman soldier gives voice to that truth.

Not as Jesus sits upon an earthly throne, but as He hangs upon a Roman cross.

And consider who is speaking. A Roman, a Gentile, someone outside Israel. We aren't told everything the centurion understood, but his declaration is remarkable.

And it brings us back to the promise made to Abraham, because God's plan of redemption was never intended to stop at Israel's borders.

4. From the Curse to the Blessing
Galatians 3:14
Now we return to Galatians. Paul has explained that Christ redeemed us from the curse of the law by becoming a curse for us. But why?

V14 gives the answer: "So that in Christ Jesus the blessing of Abraham might come to the Gentiles, so that we might receive the promised Spirit through faith."

Now we're beginning to see the Bible Arc come together. The curse, the cross, Abraham, the nations and the Holy Spirit. They're all connected.

Go back to Genesis 12. God called Abraham and promised to make him into a great nation. He promised to bless him, and through him all the families of the earth would be blessed.

We've returned to that promise repeatedly throughout this series because, after Eden, the flood and Babel, God begins revealing how His saving purposes will unfold.

But how can sinful people receive the blessing of a holy God? How can the descendants of Adam escape the consequences of Adam's rebellion? And how can Israel itself, having repeatedly broken the covenant, become the means of blessing to others?

The answer is Jesus Christ.

The promised descendant of Abraham. The faithful Son. The true and obedient King. The One who does what Adam failed to do, who remains faithful where Israel repeatedly failed, and who bears the curse that disobedience deserves.

The cross is the fulfilment of promises and patterns developing since Genesis. The promised offspring of Genesis 3:15, the blessing promised to Abraham, the Passover lamb, the sacrificial system and the suffering Servant of Isaiah 53 all point us towards Christ.

What blessing does Paul mean?

Now, this is important because the word "blessing" is frequently misunderstood. We hear people talk about God's blessing as though it automatically means financial success, perfect health or comfortable circumstances.

But look carefully at verse 14. Through Christ, the blessing of Abraham comes to the Gentiles, and through faith we receive the promised Spirit.

The Holy Spirit.

Paul hasn't introduced the Spirit as an afterthought. Earlier, in Galatians 3:2, he asked the Galatians a simple question. How did you receive the Spirit? Was it through works of the law, or through hearing the gospel with faith?

They knew the answer. They'd received the Spirit when they believed. They hadn't earned His presence by becoming obedient enough. They heard the gospel, believed in Christ, and God gave them His Spirit.

Now Paul explains why that was possible. Christ bore the curse so that the promised blessing might come to the nations. And that blessing includes God's own Spirit dwelling within His redeemed people.

Think about what that means. We aren't simply forgiven criminals who are told to go away and behave ourselves. Through Christ, we're welcomed into God's family.

Paul develops this in Galatians 4:4–6. God sent His Son, born of woman, born under the law, to redeem those under the law so that we might receive adoption as sons. And because we are His children, God has sent the Spirit of His Son into our hearts, crying, "Abba! Father!"

That's extraordinary. The Son was born under the law. He obeyed where we disobeyed. He bore the curse. And now people from the nations are welcomed into God's family through faith in Him.

Not merely tolerated. Not kept at a distance. Adopted, and given His Spirit.

Christ doesn't simply remove our condemnation. He brings us into a new relationship with God.

The blessing isn't freedom from every hardship

Now, some people take Galatians 3:13 and teach that because Jesus bore the curse, Christians should never experience sickness, poverty or suffering.

But that's not Paul's argument. Paul himself experienced beatings, imprisonment, persecution and weakness, and yet he belonged to Christ.

The New Testament never promises that becoming a Christian removes us from every consequence of living in a fallen world. What it promises is something much greater.

Romans 8:1 says, "There is therefore now no condemnation for those who are in Christ Jesus."

No condemnation. Not less condemnation. Not condemnation held in reserve until you make another mistake. No condemnation.

So perhaps someone listening today is living with illness, grief or circumstances they would never have chosen. Please don't assume your suffering means God has placed you under some hidden curse.

Remember Job. His friends insisted his suffering must be explained by his wrongdoing, and they were wrong.

Suffering, by itself, doesn't prove divine condemnation. And for those who belong to Christ, the condemnation of sin has been borne by their Saviour.

One day, when Christ returns, every remaining effect of the fall will be removed. Death will be no more, neither will mourning, crying or pain. But until then, we live by faith, and the Holy Spirit is God's gift to His redeemed people, the guarantee of the inheritance still to come.

From Babel to the nations

Now think back to Babel. Humanity united in rebellion, trying to build a name for itself. God scattered the nations. But immediately afterwards, in Genesis 12, God promised Abraham that through him all the families of the earth would be blessed.

And now, through Christ, that promise is being fulfilled.

People from every nation can come to God. Not because they share one language, not because they belong to one earthly nation, but because they're united to Jesus Christ through faith.

At Pentecost, the Holy Spirit is poured out, and the mighty works of God are proclaimed in many languages. The nations scattered at Babel are being gathered into one people through the gospel.

And Revelation 7 shows us the destination: a great multitude from every nation, tribe, people and language standing before the throne and before the Lamb.

Do you see it? Genesis begins with humanity under the consequences of the fall. Revelation ends with the curse removed. Genesis shows humanity driven out of God's presence. Revelation shows redeemed humanity dwelling with God.

And between those two stands the cross of Jesus Christ.

The King bore the curse so that the nations might receive the blessing.

→Living in the light of the cross

Now, we've seen what happened at Calvary and what it means, but the cross demands a response.

First, stop trying to justify yourself. There's something deeply rooted in the human heart that wants to earn acceptance. Even in church, we can measure ourselves by how often we attend, how much we give, how long we've served or how much Scripture we know.

Those things may be good in their proper place, but none can remove our guilt. If righteousness could come through the law, Paul says in Galatians 2:21, then Christ died for no purpose.

The cross declares that our need was far greater than self-improvement. We needed redemption, and Christ accomplished what we could never accomplish.

Second, stop living as though the cross wasn't enough. Some Christians genuinely believe in Jesus but live as though His sacrifice wasn't quite sufficient.

Perhaps there's something in your past you deeply regret. A relationship you damaged, an act of betrayal, a decision that still troubles you years later. And you think, "Surely God can't forgive that."

Listen carefully. If you're in Christ, your confidence doesn't rest in how thoroughly you've punished yourself for your past. It rests in what Jesus has accomplished.

That doesn't remove the need to confess sin or make amends where possible. But forgiveness isn't something we purchase through years of self-condemnation. Christ has borne the curse. The cross is sufficient.

And finally, live as someone who has been redeemed. In Galatians 5, Paul tells us that Christ has set us free. But freedom isn't permission to return to slavery to sin. It's freedom to walk by the Spirit, to love one another, to serve and to live as people who belong to Christ.

There's an enormous difference between trying to obey God so that He'll accept us and obeying God because we've already been accepted through Christ.

One is an attempt to earn grace. The other is the fruit of grace.

Conclusion: The King Who Bore Our Curse
Let's return to where we began. Last time, we watched Jesus enter Jerusalem on a donkey. The promised King, humble, righteous and bringing salvation.

Now we've seen Him standing before Pilate, mocked by Roman soldiers, crowned with thorns, nailed to a cross and finally crying out into the darkness, "My God, my God, why have you forsaken me?"

From a human perspective, it looks like complete defeat. The King has died, His followers are scattered, and His enemies appear to have won.

Mark 15 concludes with Jesus being placed in a tomb. Joseph of Arimathea takes His body, wraps it in linen and lays it in a tomb cut from the rock. A stone is rolled across the entrance.

But death will not have the final word. The resurrection is coming, and it will vindicate the One who was condemned, declaring His victory over sin and death.

But before we leave Calvary, remember one particular scene.

Barabbas.

A guilty man walked free while the innocent King went to the cross.

That scene gives us a glimpse of the extraordinary mercy of God. Except our problem is far greater than anything Barabbas faced before Pilate. We've sinned against the holy God who made us, and no amount of religious effort can remove that guilt.

But Christ has done what we could never do. He obeyed where we disobeyed. He bore the curse our sins deserved. And through Him, the blessing promised to Abraham comes to the nations.

So don't trust your own righteousness. Don't trust your religious record. Don't trust your ability to repair yourself.

Repent and believe in the Lord Jesus Christ. Not by repeating some religious formula and assuming the words themselves have saved you, but by turning from your sin and entrusting yourself to the Saviour who died for sinners.

Because the King who entered Jerusalem in humility was lifted up on a Roman cross, not because He had lost His kingdom, but because He had come to redeem His people.

The King bore the curse so that we could inherit the promise.

And through His death and resurrection, sinners can be reconciled to God.

That is the good news of Jesus Christ.

Closing Prayer
Father, we thank You for the Lord Jesus Christ. We confess that we've sinned against You and fallen short of Your glory. We could never establish our own righteousness before You, but thank You that You did not leave us in our guilt.

Thank You for sending Your Son, the promised King and spotless Lamb of God. Lord Jesus, we praise You for willingly bearing the curse our sins deserved. Thank You for Your perfect obedience, Your sacrifice upon the cross and Your victory over death.
Forgive us when we trust our own goodness or live as though Your sacrifice were insufficient. Teach us to rest in the righteousness that comes through faith in Christ.
May we never lose our wonder at the cross, and may we proclaim Christ faithfully to the nations.
In Jesus' name. Amen.
