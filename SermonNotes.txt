A New Heart and a New Spirit
Text: Ezekiel 36–37
Focus: Regeneration through the Spirit
Introduction: A King Needs a People

Last time, in Zechariah 6, we saw a strange and wonderful picture. Joshua the high priest was crowned, and through him Zechariah pointed beyond Joshua to the Branch.
“Behold, the man whose name is the Branch… It is he who shall build the temple of the LORD.”
The coming Messiah would do what no ordinary king or priest could do. He would be both King and Priest. He would sit upon His throne, build God's temple and bring those who were far away near.
We saw that fulfilled in Jesus Christ: the crucified, risen and reigning Priest-King who is building His church.
But that raises another question:
What sort of people is this King going to rule? What sort of people make up the temple He is building?
Because Israel's problem was never simply that they needed a better king.
They had been given kings. They had been given priests. They had the Law, the temple, sacrifices, prophets and covenant promises.
And still they rebelled.
Put sinful people under a good law and they break it. Give them a temple and they can turn worship into empty religion. Give them a land and they fill it with idols.
The problem goes deeper. The problem is the heart.
So if God's promised King is going to have a faithful people, God must do something within them that they cannot do for themselves.

1. The Problem Is Deeper Than Exile
Ezekiel 36:16–21
Israel is in exile because of persistent covenant rebellion.
“When the house of Israel lived in their own land, they defiled it by their ways and their deeds.”
The exile wasn't an accident. God had given them the land, His covenant and had placed His name among them. Yet they polluted the land through idolatry, violence and rebellion. So God scattered them among the nations.
But then something else happened. Verse 20 says:
“But when they came to the nations, wherever they came, they profaned my holy name.”
The nations looked at Israel and effectively said, “These are the LORD's people, and yet they had to leave His land.”
Israel's sin had dragged God's name through the mud.
And notice what God does not say. He doesn't say, “They just need better circumstances.” He doesn't say, “If I get them away from Babylon everything will be fine.”
Because the fundamental problem isn't Babylon. The problem is the human heart.
That's been the problem since Eden. Cain had a heart problem. The generation before the flood had a heart problem. Babel was a heart problem. Israel's wilderness rebellion was a heart problem. Their idolatry in the land was a heart problem.
Changing geography doesn't cure sin.
~Metaphor~
You can move a diseased plant into a beautiful new garden, but if the disease is in the roots, changing the soil around it won't solve the problem.
Israel needs something deeper than relocation. They need God to deal with the root.
~Application~
We often make exactly the same mistake. We imagine, “If my circumstances changed, I'd be different.” If I had a different job, a different relationship, a different church, more money, less pressure, better health, then I'd be patient. Then I'd be faithful. Then I'd really follow God.
Circumstances matter. But Scripture keeps taking us deeper.
Jesus says that evil comes from within, out of the heart.
Our greatest problem isn't simply the world around us. It is the sin within us. Which means salvation must go deeper than changing our circumstances.
God must change us.

2. For the Sake of His Name
Ezekiel 36:22–24
Then God says something surprising:
“It is not for your sake, O house of Israel, that I am about to act, but for the sake of my holy name.”
That destroys any idea that Israel has earned restoration.
God isn't looking at them and saying, “You've improved enough now. You've finally proved yourselves.”
No. Their salvation begins in God, not them.
God acts because He is faithful to His own character, His covenant and His name. That is grace.
Then comes the promise:
“I will take you from the nations and gather you from all the countries and bring you into your own land.”
Notice the repeated subject throughout this section: I will.
I will take you. I will gather you. I will cleanse you. I will give you. I will remove. I will put My Spirit within you.
The salvation described here is God's work from beginning to end.
Application
That's where Christian salvation begins as well. Not, “I cleaned myself up.” Not, “I became religious enough.” Not, “I found something good inside myself.”
Paul says: “But God, being rich in mercy…”
Those may be two of the most important words in Scripture.
But God.
We were not climbing towards Him. He came towards us.

3. Clean Water
Ezekiel 36:25
God promises: “I will sprinkle clean water on you, and you shall be clean from all your uncleannesses.”
Before He speaks of the new heart, He speaks of cleansing, because regeneration cannot be separated from the problem of guilt and defilement.
Israel has become unclean through sin. God promises to cleanse them.
“From all your idols I will cleanse you.”
The idols they had clung to, the sins that had polluted them and the rebellion that had brought them into exile. God Himself will deal with it.
This echoes the cleansing rituals of the Old Testament. But those ritual washings pointed beyond themselves. Water could wash skin. It could symbolically cleanse ceremonial impurity. But ordinary water could never wash guilt from the human heart.
David understood that. After his sin he prayed:
“Wash me thoroughly from my iniquity, and cleanse me from my sin.”
He knew his problem required a cleansing only God could provide.
And ultimately that cleansing comes through Christ.
“The blood of Jesus his Son cleanses us from all sin.”
Christianity doesn't say, “Your past doesn't matter.” It says something far better:"Your sin can be forgiven."
There really is guilt. There really is uncleanness. But there really is cleansing.
Not because we wash ourselves, but because God has provided cleansing through Christ.

4. From Stone to Flesh
Ezekiel 36:26–27
Now we reach the heart of the promise:
“And I will give you a new heart, and a new spirit I will put within you.”
Then God explains:
“And I will remove the heart of stone from your flesh and give you a heart of flesh.”
Notice what God doesn't say.
He doesn't say, “I will polish your stone heart.” He doesn't say, “I will educate your stone heart.” He doesn't even say, “I will encourage your stone heart.”
“I will remove it.”
The problem isn't that sinful humanity needs a little spiritual motivation. The heart is resistant to God. Hard. Unresponsive. Stone.
And God promises something only God can do. He gives a new heart.
~Metaphor~
Imagine a sculptor standing over a block of stone. You can paint it, decorate it, polish it and write religious words across it.
But it remains stone.
God's promise is not cosmetic renovation.
He removes the stone and gives living flesh.
And then: “I will put my Spirit within you.”
That changes everything.
God doesn't merely give His people new rules. Israel already had God's law. The problem wasn't lack of information. They knew what God commanded. Their problem was that their hearts continually resisted Him.
So the new covenant promise is greater than another command written on stone. God places His Spirit within His people.
And look at the result:
“And cause you to walk in my statutes and be careful to obey my rules.”
They do not obey in order to receive the new heart. God gives the new heart, and obedience begins to follow.

OBEDIENCE IT THE FRUIT OF REGENERATION, NOT IT’S CAUSE

And this is where we need to be careful about how we speak about conversion.
A prayer can be a genuine expression of repentance and faith. There is nothing wrong with someone crying out to God for mercy. The problem comes when we make the prayer itself the thing that saves, or when we give someone assurance simply because they can remember saying certain words.
Someone may say, “I know I’m saved because I prayed a prayer twenty years ago.” But Ezekiel does not tell us to look for a prayer twenty years ago. He tells us what God does when He saves.

He gives a new heart. He puts His Spirit within us. He causes us to walk in His ways.
 “Did I once say the right words?” The question is, “Has God made me alive?”
•    Has my relationship with sin changed? 
•    Is there repentance where once I simply defended myself? Is there a growing love for Christ? 
•    Is there a desire to obey Him, however imperfectly?
•    When I sin, is there something within me that now grieves over it and draws me back to Christ?

Not perfection. This passage does not teach instant sinlessness. Christians stumble. Christians can go through terrible seasons. Christians still battle the flesh.
But there is life.
The evidence of regeneration is not that I once made a decision. It is that God has begun a work in me that I could never begin in myself.
And notice the order again. We do not change so that God will give us a new heart. God gives the new heart, and change begins to follow.
~Application~
That's the difference between Christianity and mere behaviour modification.
Religion can tell you, “Try harder. Do better. Control yourself.” And God's law certainly tells us what righteousness looks like.
But the gospel goes deeper.
God changes the heart from which behaviour flows.
The Christian isn't someone who has simply adopted a better moral system.
Something supernatural has happened.

God's Spirit has made them alive.

5. Can These Bones Live?
Ezekiel 37:1–10
Then God gives Ezekiel a picture of what He has just promised.
The Spirit carries Ezekiel into a valley, and everywhere he looks there are bones.
“There were very many on the surface of the valley.”
And Ezekiel notices something else.
“They were very dry.”
They haven't died recently. There is no suggestion of life remaining. No pulse. No breath. No possibility.
And God asks Ezekiel:
“Son of man, can these bones live?”
Ezekiel gives perhaps the wisest answer possible:
“O Lord GOD, you know.”
Humanly? No.
Bones don't recover. Dead people don't rehabilitate themselves.
But the question isn't really about the ability of the bones. It's about the power of God.
Then God tells Ezekiel:
“Prophesy over these bones.”
He speaks God's Word. Suddenly there is a sound. A rattling. Bones begin coming together, bone to bone. Sinews appear. Flesh covers them. Skin forms.
Ez looks. Bodies everywhere.
But verse 8 says: “There was no breath in them.”
They have form. Structure. Everything appears to be present.
But there is no life.
Then God says: “Prophesy to the breath.”
The Hebrew word ruach can mean wind, breath or spirit.
Ezekiel cries: “Come from the four winds, O breath, and breathe on these slain, that they may live.”
And the breath enters them.
“And they lived and stood on their feet, an exceedingly great army.”
Word and Spirit
Notice how God brings life.
His Word is proclaimed and His Spirit gives life.
Word and Spirit.
¬ Ezekiel  manufacturing resurrection. His job is to speak what God has said.
God supplies the life.
~Application~
That is why the church never outgrows the proclamation of God's Word.
We cannot regenerate anyone. We cannot argue someone into spiritual life. We cannot entertain them into the kingdom. We cannot manufacture conversion through atmosphere, music, pressure, raised hands or repeated words.

We proclaim Christ and depend upon the Spirit of God to do what only He can do.

And we also need to be careful not to declare life too quickly.

A skeleton can be arranged beautifully.

It can sit in a church building. It can know the songs. It can know Christian vocabulary. It can remember the night it went forward, raised a hand or repeated a prayer.
But none of those things, by themselves, are resurrection.
The great question in Ezekiel’s valley is not:
“Did something happen once?”
It is: “Is there breath?”
Has the Spirit of God brought life?
That does not mean we spend our lives anxiously examining ourselves until we find enough evidence to feel worthy. Our eyes ultimately belong on Christ.
But neither should we give ourselves false assurance because of a ceremony, a decision or words spoken years ago while our present life remains completely indifferent to Christ.
Speak to the bones. Pray for the breath.
Only God gives life.

6. “Our Hope Is Lost”
Ezekiel 37:11–14
God then explains the vision:
“Son of man, these bones are the whole house of Israel.”
So we mustn't rip the valley of dry bones out of its original context. Its immediate message is to Israel in exile.
They are saying:
“Our bones are dried up, and our hope is lost; we are indeed cut off.”
They think the story is over. The nation is shattered. Jerusalem has fallen. The temple is gone. The people are scattered.
From their perspective, God's promises appear to have died.
And God says:
“Behold, I will open your graves and raise you from your graves, O my people.”
The language is resurrection language applied first to the restoration of God's apparently dead covenant people.
God will bring them home.
But again, the language goes far beyond simply saying, “I'm arranging transport back to Jerusalem.”
He describes restoration as life from death.
Verse 14:
“And I will put my Spirit within you, and you shall live.”
That's Ezekiel 36 again. New heart. New Spirit. Life.
The deepest restoration God promises is not merely returning people to a land.
It is God dwelling within His people by His Spirit.

~Application~
Sometimes we look at ourselves and think, “There is too much wrong with me.”
Or we look at someone else and think, “They will never come to Christ.”
Or even at the church and think, “Our culture is too far gone.”
Ezekiel sees an entire valley of dry bones.
God does not ask, “Is this difficult?”
He asks: “Can these bones live?”
Not by themselves. But God raises the dead.

7. Jesus and the New Birth
John 3:1–8
Centuries later, a religious leader comes to Jesus at night.
Nicodemus.
A teacher of Israel.
Jesus tells him: “Unless one is born again he cannot see the kingdom of God.”
Nicodemus is confused.
So Jesus says: “Unless one is born of water and the Spirit, he cannot enter the kingdom of God.”
That language should sound familiar.
Water. Spirit. New life.
Ezekiel 36.
Nicodemus isn't merely being introduced to an interesting new Christian phrase called being born again. Jesus is pointing him back to what God had promised Israel.
Cleansing. A new heart. The Spirit within.
And Jesus asks him: “Are you the teacher of Israel and yet you do not understand these things?”
He should have known that entry into God's kingdom requires something more than ancestry, religious knowledge or moral effort.
You need new birth.
And that new life ultimately comes through Christ.
John the Baptist had already said of Jesus: “He will baptize you with the Holy Spirit.”
Jesus dies. Jesus rises. Jesus ascends.
And at Pentecost the risen Christ pours out the promised Spirit.
The promise Ezekiel saw now begins reaching out to the nations.
Dead sinners are made alive. Stone hearts become living hearts. People who were far from God become His dwelling place by the Spirit.

Conclusion: From Stone to Breath
Look at the movement through these chapters.
We are unclean, and God says, “I will cleanse you.”
We have a heart of stone, and God says, “I will give you a new heart.”
We are powerless to obey, and God says, “I will put my Spirit within you.”
We are dry bones, and God says, “You shall live.”
That's salvation.
We don't need God merely to make our stone hearts slightly softer. We need a new heart.
We don't need Him to teach dead bones how to behave.
We need life.
Following Jesus ¬ God standing over a valley of corpses shouting, “Try harder!”
The bones can't try harder. They're dead.
The gospel is God speaking life where there was death.
And this is where Christ stands at the centre of everything Ezekiel promised.
Jesus took our uncleanness upon Himself. Jesus bore our judgment at the cross. Jesus went into death. And Jesus came out of the grave alive.
The risen Christ gives His Spirit to His people.
Paul says:“If anyone is in Christ, he is a new creation.”
And: “Even when we were dead in our trespasses, [God] made us alive together with Christ.”
Maybe you’ve spent years trying to renovate the stone. Trying to become good enough, religious enough or respectable enough.
Or perhaps your confidence before God rests on something that happened years ago.
“I prayed the prayer.”
“I went forward.”
“I raised my hand.”
“I asked Jesus into my heart.”
And perhaps somebody told you, “Never question it again. You prayed the prayer, so you are saved.”
But your hope is not a prayer you prayed.
Your hope is Jesus Christ.
A prayer cannot cleanse your sin. A decision cannot remove a heart of stone. Walking to the front of a church cannot raise dry bones.
Only God saves.
Only Christ can cleanse.
Only the Spirit gives life.
So do not look back twenty years to a formula and use it to make peace with twenty years of indifference to Jesus.
Look to Christ now.
Repent and believe the gospel.
Come to the One who died for sinners and rose from the grave. Trust Him, not your prayer, not your goodness and not your religious history.
And if God has made you alive, that does not mean you will be perfect. There will still be sin to fight, failures to confess and weakness that drives you again and again to grace.
But there will be life.
There will be a new direction. New desires. A new relationship with sin. A love for Christ that was not there before. A struggle against things you once simply accepted.
Dead bones do not struggle with being dead. Living people struggle because they are alive.
So if you see that struggle in yourself, do not immediately conclude that the struggle proves you are lost. Sometimes the very battle is evidence that there is now life where once there was none.
And if there is no life, the answer is not to manufacture some.
Come to Christ.
You cannot regenerate yourself. You cannot breathe life into your own bones. But the God who spoke creation into existence still gives life to the dead.
And if you already belong to Christ, remember what has happened to you. Your Christianity is not ultimately built upon your ability to keep yourself spiritually alive. The Spirit who gave life continues His work within you.
So when you see dry bones, don’t despair. When you see hardness, don’t stop praying. When the gospel seems powerless, keep proclaiming Christ.
Speak the Word. Pray for the breath.
Because the God who says:
“I will put my Spirit within you”
also says:
“And you shall live.”

Prayer
Thank God that salvation is His work from beginning to end.
Confess the sin and hardness of our hearts.
Thank Jesus for cleansing us through His blood.
Praise God for giving new hearts rather than merely improving the old.
Thank the Holy Spirit for bringing spiritually dead people to life.
Ask Him to continue producing obedience and holiness within us.
Pray for those who presently seem like dry bones, that God would bring them to life.
Ask that the church would faithfully proclaim Christ while depending upon the Spirit.
Amen.
