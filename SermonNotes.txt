The Kingdom Is at Hand
Matthew 5:1-12
For a long time now we have been following what we called the Arc, tracing the story of Scripture from creation, through the fall, Abraham and Israel, the kings, the exile and the prophets, watching all those lines gradually move towards Christ.
We saw promises made that Israel could never finally fulfil. We saw kings who could not fix the human heart, sacrifices that had to be offered again and again, exile brought by sin, and prophets looking forward to something better: a new covenant, a new heart, God's Spirit within His people, and a King who would finally put things right.
And eventually the Arc brought us to Jesus.
Over the last few weeks we have slowed right down around Him. Last week, in John 1, John the Baptist looked up, saw Jesus coming towards him and said, “Behold, the Lamb of God, who takes away the sin of the world.” We saw why He had come. He is the Lamb God provides, the One who deals with sin, the One who baptises with the Holy Spirit, the Son of God.
But once the King has arrived, another question follows naturally.
What does life under this King actually look like?
Because Matthew tells us that when Jesus began His public ministry, His announcement was very simple: “Repent, for the kingdom of heaven is at hand.” (Matt 4:17)
The kingdom is at hand because the King is here.
Jesus begins calling disciples. He goes through Galilee teaching in the synagogues, proclaiming the gospel of the kingdom and healing the sick. Crowds begin to gather from Galilee, the Decapolis, Jerusalem, Judea and beyond the Jordan. Something is happening.
@ Matt 5:1, “Seeing the crowds, he went up on the mountain, and when he sat down, his disciples came to him. And he opened his mouth and taught them.”
And then Jesus begins: “Blessed are the poor in spirit, for theirs is the kingdom of heaven.”
These are some of the most familiar words Jesus ever spoke. We call them the Beatitudes. But familiarity can work against us. We recognise the words so quickly that we can stop hearing how strange they actually are. “Blessed are the poor in spirit.” “Blessed are those who mourn.” “Blessed are the meek.” “Blessed are those who are persecuted.”
That is not normally how the world describes people who are doing well.
Every country has a culture. There are things you grow up with that seem completely normal because everybody around you does them. Then you travel somewhere else and discover that apparently the rest of the world has failed to learn the correct way of doing things. For me as a driving instructor, we could begin with the fact that vast sections of the world have somehow decided to drive on the wrong side of the road.
Culture is like that. Food, language, humour, manners, values. You can be so immersed in your own culture that you don't even notice it until you encounter another one. There is that famous moment in 

The Wizard of Oz: “Toto, I've a feeling we're not in Kansas anymore.” Dorothy has crossed into another world and suddenly the assumptions she brought with her don't quite work. Or, in a much less profound way, Peter Kay discovering something apparently exotic and reacting, “Garlic bread?!” One person's normal can sound completely strange to somebody else.

I think there is something like that happening in the Beatitudes.

The Arc has brought us to the King. Last week John pointed at Him and said, “Behold.” Now Jesus begins telling us what His kingdom is like. And almost immediately we experience culture shock.

If I asked you tonight to describe the blessed life, what would you say? Good health? Enough money? Security? Success? Influence? Our culture has its own beatitudes. Blessed are the successful. Blessed are the confident. Blessed are those who get noticed. Blessed are those who can control the outcome. Blessed are those who win.

Then Jesus sits down on a mountain and says, “Blessed are the poor in spirit.”

We have crossed a border.

We are hearing the values of another kingdom.
The Beatitudes are not eight unrelated pieces of advice. They are not eight ways of improving ourselves, and they are certainly not eight things we achieve in order to persuade God to let us into His kingdom. Jesus has already said, “The kingdom of heaven is at hand.” The kingdom comes because the King has come. Now Jesus begins teaching His disciples what is normal in His kingdom.

Before we go through them, there is one word we need to understand: “Blessed.” Sometimes people paraphrase that as “happy.” There is some truth in that, but “happy” can give us the wrong impression. Jesus is not saying mourning or persecution feel pleasant. The word carries the idea of being fortunate, favoured, in an enviable position. Jesus is declaring God's verdict. The world may look at somebody and say, “I wouldn't want their life.” Jesus can look at exactly the same person and say, “Blessed.” Blessing is ultimately determined by the King, not by the culture surrounding us.

1. The kingdom begins with empty hands - Matt 5:3
@ v 3, “Blessed are the poor in spirit, for theirs is the kingdom of heaven.” The first word Jesus uses to describe kingdom people is poor. He doesn't begin, “Blessed are the spiritually impressive.” He doesn't say, “Blessed are those who have got their lives together.” He says, “Blessed are the poor in spirit.”

Immediately the culture of this kingdom sounds different. Our culture admires the self-made person, the one who says, “I did it myself. I didn't need anybody.” Jesus begins with people who say almost the opposite: “I need.”

Jesus is not romanticising material poverty here. Matthew gives us the phrase “poor in spirit.” The idea is spiritual poverty, recognising our complete dependence upon God. The poor in spirit know they cannot walk into God's presence waving their spiritual achievements in front of Him.

We don't come before God with our CV: “Lord, I went to church. I served. I gave. I preached. I knew the right theology. I wasn't as bad as him.” The poor in spirit have stopped playing that game. Their hands are empty.

Jesus gives us a wonderful picture later in Luke's Gospel. Two men go into the temple to pray. One is a Pharisee, and his prayer is essentially a spiritual CV: “God, I thank you that I am not like other men.” He lists what he doesn't do, then lists the religious things he does. He is standing before God with his hands full of himself. Then Jesus describes a tax collector who will not even lift his eyes to heaven. He beats his chest and says, “God, be merciful to me, a sinner.” (Lk 18:9-14)

That is poverty of spirit. No bargaining. No comparison. No pretending. “God, I need mercy.” And Jesus says it is that man who goes home justified.

The first doorway into kingdom life is not achievement. It is need. That is offensive to human pride because we like to contribute something. If somebody gives us an expensive gift, our instinct may be, “I need to get them something.” We want to balance the books. If we bring that instinct into salvation, we start thinking, “Surely I contribute something.”

But Jesus begins here: “Blessed are the poor in spirit.” The gospel begins when I realise I cannot rescue myself. I cannot forgive my own sins. I cannot change my own heart. I cannot make myself righteous before a holy God. I come with empty hands.

Then look at the promise: “for theirs is the kingdom of heaven.” Not, “Eventually, if you prove yourself.” Theirs is the kingdom. The people who know they have nothing are given a kingdom. Jesus says elsewhere, “Fear not, little flock, for it is your Father's good pleasure to give you the kingdom.” (Lk 12:32)

God gives. We receive. That is the culture of grace. In the kingdoms we build, status is often achieved. In this kingdom, citizenship is received.

Spiritual poverty is not spiritual hopelessness. There is a huge difference between saying, “I have nothing,” and saying, “There is nothing.” The Christian says, “I have nothing that can purchase my salvation, but God has given everything I need in Christ.” Empty hands can receive.

And we don't begin with poverty of spirit and then grow out of it. Sometimes we act as though we become Christians by grace and then gradually become less dependent upon grace. At the beginning: “Lord, I desperately need You.” Twenty years later: “I've got this now.” No. The Christian life begins with dependence and continues with dependence.

Paul asks in 1 Cor 4:7, “What do you have that you did not receive?” Your breath, abilities, opportunities, salvation, spiritual gifts, knowledge. Everything is gift.

So the first thing Jesus teaches us about the culture of His kingdom is this: need is not something we have to hide from God. It is where we meet Him. Blessed are those who come with empty hands, because theirs is the kingdom of heaven.


2. The kingdom does not pretend everything is fine - Matt 5:4
@ v 4, “Blessed are those who mourn, for they shall be comforted.” That may be even stranger than the first Beatitude.

There is a version of our culture that does not really know what to do with grief. We are good at distraction. Keep busy. Keep scrolling. Find something funny. Buy something. Watch something. Move on. There can even be a version of Christianity that makes people feel guilty for being sad, as though mature Christians must be permanently cheerful.

But biblical Christianity does not require us to pretend everything is fine when everything is not fine.

Jesus Himself wept. At the grave of Lazarus, Jesus knew resurrection was coming. He knew Lazarus would soon walk out of that tomb, and still, “Jesus wept.” (Jn 11:35) Hope did not make Him emotionally numb.

The Bible gives us entire Psalms of lament: “How long, O LORD?” “Why have you forgotten me?” “My God, my God, why have you forsaken me?” Scripture does not hide pain. That itself is part of the culture of God's kingdom. We tell the truth about what is broken.

So what are those who mourn mourning? Jesus doesn't specify one single object. Personal sin can certainly belong here. When we begin seeing ourselves honestly before God, poverty of spirit can naturally lead into mourning. There is a kind of repentance that says, “Yes, I know I shouldn't have done that,” and another that actually grieves because our sin is against God. Paul speaks about “godly grief” producing repentance. (2 Cor 7:10)

But kingdom people also mourn what sin has done to God's world. Families breaking. People dying. Children suffering. Wars. Abuse. Addiction. Loneliness. Disease. People made in God's image treating one another as disposable. Something is wrong with the world.

Christianity does not answer that by saying, “Look on the bright side.” Christianity says, “Yes. Something really is wrong.” The Bible calls death an enemy. (1 Cor 15:26) There are times when mourning is exactly the right response to living in a broken world.

But Jesus does not stop with mourning: “for they shall be comforted.” There is a promise attached to the grief.

There is comfort now. Paul calls God “the Father of mercies and God of all comfort.” (2 Cor 1:3) The church is supposed to carry one another's burdens. But the promise reaches beyond the present.

Rev 21 gives us that extraordinary picture: “He will wipe away every tear from their eyes, and death shall be no more.” Christian hope is not merely that God will make us better at coping with death. Christian hope is resurrection. It is not that one day evil won't bother us. It is that one day evil will be judged and removed.

So Christian mourning always has a horizon. Paul says we do not grieve “as others do who have no hope.” (1 Thess 4:13) Notice what he doesn't say. He doesn't say we do not grieve. We do. But grief with hope is different from grief without hope.

Maybe somebody listening tonight is mourning a person, a relationship, something that happened years ago, your own failure, or what somebody did to you. Jesus does not say, “Pretend that doesn't hurt.” He says, “Blessed are those who mourn, for they shall be comforted.”

In the culture of this kingdom, tears are not evidence that God has abandoned you. The King sees them, and mourning does not get the final word.

3. The meek don't have to seize the kingdom - Matt 5:5
@ v 5, “Blessed are the meek, for they shall inherit the earth.” Meekness is one of those words that can sound weak in modern English. We might hear timid, passive, someone who gets pushed around.

But biblical meekness is not weakness.

Jesus describes Himself as “gentle and lowly in heart.” (Matt 11:29) Nobody reading the Gospels could call Jesus weak. He confronts hypocrisy. He overturns tables in the temple. He stands before Pilate knowing Pilate has political power over Him, and Jesus is still the one controlling the conversation.

Meekness is not inability to act. It is strength that does not need constantly to assert itself.

Again, that sounds foreign because we live in a culture where visibility and self-promotion are almost necessities. Make yourself heard. Build the profile. Protect the brand. Never let anybody think they got the better of you.

Then Jesus says, “Blessed are the meek.”

Jesus' words echo Ps 37:11: “The meek shall inherit the land.” The context matters. Psalm 37 asks why wicked people seem to prosper. Why do people who cheat sometimes get ahead? Why do cruel people appear to win? The Psalm repeatedly tells God's people: do not fret, trust in the LORD, be still before Him, wait patiently for Him.

That is where meekness comes from. The meek do not have to seize everything for themselves because they trust God with the outcome.

There are legitimate times to speak and legitimate rights to defend. Meekness does not mean becoming somebody's doormat. But kingdom people do not have to spend their whole lives fighting for position. We don't have to win every argument, respond to every insult or correct every person who misunderstands us.

Jesus Himself was falsely accused. Peter says, “When he was reviled, he did not revile in return,” because “he continued entrusting himself to him who judges justly.” (1 Pet 2:23)

There is the secret of meekness: entrusting yourself to the One who judges justly. If I believe I am the final judge of my life, I have to defend myself constantly. But if there is a righteous Judge, I can sometimes let things go. I can choose not to retaliate. I can forgive. I can serve without needing recognition.

Then Jesus says, “They shall inherit the earth.”

The people who refuse to grasp for everything will inherit everything.

Human empires are built by taking land. Jesus tells His followers they receive an inheritance. You earn wages. You inherit because you belong to a family. And this is bigger than “one day you go to heaven.” Jesus says they inherit the earth. The biblical hope is resurrection and renewed creation.

The culture around us says, “Take what you can now.” Jesus says, “Trust your Father. Your inheritance is safe.”

4. What are you hungry for? - Matt 5:6
@ v 6, “Blessed are those who hunger and thirst for righteousness, for they shall be satisfied.”

Jesus uses two basic human appetites: hunger and thirst. Most of us say, “I'm starving,” when we mean we haven't eaten for a few hours. Real hunger dominates your thinking. Real thirst takes over. Your body says, “I need this.”

Jesus says that is what kingdom people are like towards righteousness. They hunger for it. They thirst for it. Not, “Righteousness would be nice if convenient.” Hunger. Thirst.

Every culture trains our appetites. Advertising does it deliberately. You didn't know you needed something until somebody showed you an advert explaining that your life was incomplete without it. Suddenly you need the new phone, new car, new game, whatever it is. Apparently humanity survived thousands of years without it, but now we can't continue until Amazon delivers it tomorrow.

Culture trains appetite. Jesus says the culture of His kingdom creates a different hunger.

Righteousness here certainly includes personal righteousness, a desire to live rightly before God. Later Jesus says, “Seek first the kingdom of God and his righteousness.” (Matt 6:33) Kingdom people don't simply ask, “How close can I get to sin without technically crossing the line?” They begin wanting what God wants.

But righteousness also has a wider dimension. We hunger to see things put right: justice, truth, faithfulness, relationships restored, the vulnerable protected, God's good order established. We pray, “Your kingdom come, your will be done, on earth as it is in heaven.”

The question becomes: what are we actually hungry for?

Appetite reveals something. People make time for what they hunger for. They spend money on it. They read about it, watch videos about it, rearrange their schedule around it. None of those things are necessarily wrong, but Jesus' question goes deeper. Do I actually hunger for righteousness? Do I want to know God more? Do I want sin out of my life? Do I care that God's name is honoured? Or am I content with enough Christianity to make me comfortable?

Sometimes our spiritual problem is not lack of information. We already know what Scripture says. Our problem is appetite.

And the answer is not to manufacture fake enthusiasm. Even hunger for righteousness is something we bring before God: “Lord, make me want what You want.”

There is a hunger in human beings that we try to feed with all sorts of things, some of them good things, but no created thing can carry the weight of being God.

The Psalmist says, “As a deer pants for flowing streams, so pants my soul for you, O God.” (Ps 42:1) Jesus says, “they shall be satisfied.”

That is another future promise. In this age, there is always more righteousness to hunger for. Paul still says, “Not that I have already obtained this or am already perfect.” (Phil 3:12) But one day the hunger will be completely satisfied. 1 Jn 3:2 says, “when he appears we shall be like him, because we shall see him as he is.”

No more battle with sin. No more divided heart. No more saying, “Why did I do that again?” No more injustice. Righteousness at home in a renewed creation.

Notice the movement through these first four Beatitudes: poor in spirit, mourning, meek, hungry. These are not people swaggering into the kingdom. They know their need. They grieve what is wrong. They trust God rather than grasping. They hunger for what only God can finally provide.

They have crossed into another culture. Things the old world may call weakness, Jesus calls blessed.

5. Mercy and a pure heart - Matt 5:7-8
@ v 7, “Blessed are the merciful, for they shall receive mercy.” Now something begins to turn outward. What happens when people who know they live by mercy begin dealing with other people?

Mercy is what happens when somebody has reason to condemn, retaliate or withhold compassion, and chooses instead to show kindness. Jesus says kingdom people are merciful because they know they live by mercy.

That clashes with a strong cultural instinct: make them pay. Somebody hurts you, hurt them back. Somebody embarrasses you, embarrass them. Somebody attacks you online, make sure your reply hits harder.

Jesus says, “Blessed are the merciful.”

Later in Matthew, Jesus tells the story of a servant who owes his king an enormous debt, one he could never repay. He begs for patience, and the king does something far greater than he asks. He forgives the debt completely.

Then that servant finds somebody who owes him a much smaller amount and demands payment. The second man pleads using almost the same words he himself had just used, but there is no mercy. He has him thrown into prison. (Matt 18:21-35)

The story is supposed to feel wrong. How can somebody who has just received staggering mercy refuse mercy to somebody else?

Jesus is not saying we earn God's mercy by being merciful. Mercy cannot be earned. The point is that people who have genuinely grasped God's mercy begin to become merciful. Grace received becomes grace extended.

That doesn't mean there are no consequences. It doesn't mean justice disappears. It doesn't mean calling evil good. Biblical mercy is not pretending somebody didn't hurt you. But mercy refuses to let vengeance become master of the heart.

Paul says, “Beloved, never avenge yourselves, but leave it to the wrath of God.” (Rom 12:19) I don't have to become the final judge because God is. That frees me to show mercy.

How do I respond when somebody fails? How quickly do I write people off? Do I secretly enjoy hearing that somebody who hurt me is suffering? When somebody genuinely repents, do I keep reminding them who they used to be?

Kingdom people remember, “I live because God has been merciful to me.”

@ v 8, “Blessed are the pure in heart, for they shall see God.”

Now Jesus goes deeper. He is not satisfied with external religion. Purity of heart includes an undivided heart, but it is broader than that. It is inward integrity, purity of motive and a life genuinely directed towards God.

Ps 24 asks, “Who shall ascend the hill of the LORD?” Answer: “He who has clean hands and a pure heart.” (Ps 24:3-4) Clean hands and pure heart. Outside and inside.

Human beings are very good at concentrating on clean hands while ignoring the heart. We can learn religious behaviour, vocabulary and appearance. We can attend church, serve, preach, sing and volunteer while looking completely respectable.

And we live in a culture especially skilled at image management. Profile pictures, status updates, carefully chosen photographs, avatars. We decide what people see.

VR makes that especially obvious. You can literally choose what people see. You can appear as almost anything.

But Jesus keeps going underneath the avatar.

What is happening in the heart?

Later in Matt 5 He will say murder begins in the heart with anger and adultery begins in the heart with lust. Jesus is not making morality easier. He is taking it deeper. The kingdom reaches into desire, motive and thought, into who we are when nobody else is watching.

That should make every one of us poor in spirit again. If righteousness were merely external, we might convince ourselves we were doing quite well. But when Jesus starts talking about the heart, we realise how much we need grace.

David prays, “Unite my heart to fear your name.” (Ps 86:11) Our hearts can become divided. Part of me wants God, part of me wants something else. Part of me wants obedience, part of me wants my own way.

“Unite my heart.”

The culture around us asks, “How do I look?” Jesus asks, “Who are you when only your Father sees?”

Then He says, “they shall see God.” The greatest promise of the gospel is not merely that we escape judgement. It is God Himself. To know Him, be with Him, see Him. Rev 22:4 says, “They will see his face.”


6. Kingdom people make peace - Matt 5:9
@ v 9, “Blessed are the peacemakers, for they shall be called sons of God.”

Notice Jesus does not say, “Blessed are the peace-lovers.” Most people like peace when peace means nobody is bothering them. He says peacemakers, people who actively pursue peace.

Sometimes what we call peace is really silence. Nobody talks about the problem. Everybody stays polite. Resentment remains underneath, but at least there is no argument. That is not necessarily peace.

Biblical peace, shalom, is more than the absence of hostility. It is wholeness, restoration, things put right. Peacemaking sometimes requires entering difficult situations rather than avoiding them.

Jesus Himself is the great Peacemaker. Rom 5:1 says, “Since we have been justified by faith, we have peace with God through our Lord Jesus Christ.” Why do we need peace with God? Because sin has created hostility. Christ does not make peace by pretending sin doesn't matter. Col 1:20 says He makes peace “by the blood of his cross.”

Real peace can be costly.

And if we belong to the Prince of Peace, peace ought to characterise us. That doesn't mean Christians never disagree. Read the New Testament. Christians disagree, sometimes strongly. The question is what we do with disagreement.

Do I need to destroy the person to defeat their argument? Do I assume the worst possible motive? Do I repeat things I haven't checked? Do I become a different person behind a keyboard?

That last one matters particularly in a VR church. It is possible to say things through a headset, Discord or social media that we would never say if the person were sitting across a table from us.

But Jesus' kingdom does not disappear when we log on.

There is no border control at the edge of Discord where we leave the Beatitudes behind.

Truth matters. Peacemaking does not mean doctrinal mush. Jesus confronts error. The apostles confront error. But Paul tells Timothy, “The Lord's servant must not be quarrelsome but kind to everyone... correcting his opponents with gentleness.” (2 Tim 2:24-25)

Truth without gentleness can become brutality. Gentleness without truth can become cowardice. Peacemaking holds truth and love together.

Sometimes peacemaking means being the first person to say, “I was wrong.” Sometimes, “I'm sorry.” Sometimes, “I misunderstood you.” Sometimes, “Can we talk about this rather than talking about each other?” Sometimes it means refusing to pass on gossip.

Peacemaking takes courage. Anybody can throw another log on a fire. It takes maturity to put one out.

Jesus says, “they shall be called sons of God.” Not because peacemaking earns adoption, but because it is family resemblance. God is the God of peace. His Son makes peace. His children become peacemakers.

You can tell which kingdom's culture they belong to.

7. When two kingdom cultures collide - Matt 5:10-12
@ v 10, “Blessed are those who are persecuted for righteousness' sake, for theirs is the kingdom of heaven.”

That is a surprising place for the Beatitudes to end. You might expect: blessed are the merciful, blessed are the peacemakers, therefore everybody will think these people are wonderful.

Jesus says no! Sometimes they will persecute you.

This is where the culture metaphor becomes serious. If you visit another country for a week, you can enjoy the differences. But if you live there, eventually you discover that culture is not only food and clothing. It is values, assumptions, what people call good and what people call shameful.

If you live according to the values of one kingdom while surrounded by another, eventually the difference becomes visible.

The values of Jesus' kingdom are not simply a slightly nicer version of the values everybody already has. Eventually kingdoms collide.

If Jesus is King, Caesar is never ultimate. If Jesus defines righteousness, the surrounding culture does not get the final word. If Jesus tells me what is true, I cannot simply change truth every time the surrounding culture changes its mind.

Following Jesus will eventually put Christians out of step.

We do need to be careful. Not every negative reaction to a Christian is persecution. If I am rude and somebody calls me rude, I am not being persecuted for righteousness. Peter makes exactly this distinction: “Let none of you suffer as a murderer or a thief or an evildoer or as a meddler.” But, “If anyone suffers as a Christian, let him not be ashamed.” (1 Pet 4:15-16)

Jesus is specific: “persecuted for righteousness' sake.”
@ v 11, He makes it even more personal: “Blessed are you when others revile you and persecute you and utter all kinds of evil against you falsely on my account.”

Notice the shift. v 10 says “for righteousness' sake.” v 11 says “on my account.”

Ultimately, the issue is Jesus. Loyalty to kingdom righteousness becomes loyalty to the King.

There is an extraordinary claim here. Jesus places Himself at the centre of faithfulness to God. He doesn't merely say, “You may suffer for believing in God.” He says, “You may suffer because of Me.”

Bonhoeffer wrote, “When Christ calls a man, he bids him come and die.” Long before Bonhoeffer, Jesus had already refused to sell cheap discipleship.

For the first disciples, the cost became very literal. Stephen was stoned. James was killed. Peter and John were arrested. Paul was repeatedly beaten and imprisoned, and early Christian tradition says he was eventually executed.

There are Christians today for whom baptism can mean losing family, conversion can mean losing employment, and gathering with other believers can carry genuine danger. We should remember that before using the word “persecution” too casually about ourselves.

But even where we have significant religious freedom, faithfulness to Jesus can cost something. Friends may not understand. Family may think you've become extreme. Honesty may cost you. Refusing to join in may cost you socially.

Jesus does not hide that from potential disciples. He never says, “Follow Me and everything becomes easier.” He tells people to count the cost.

And yet He still says, “Blessed.”

That means suffering cannot cancel blessing. Opposition cannot revoke citizenship.

@ v 12, “Rejoice and be glad.” Why? Not because pain is pleasant. Not because false accusations don't hurt. Jesus Himself was in anguish in Gethsemane. There is nothing spiritual about trying to make people hate you.

So why rejoice? “For your reward is great in heaven, for so they persecuted the prophets who were before you.”

Jesus changes the horizon.

If this life is all there is, persecution is simply loss. But if the kingdom is real, loss is not final. People can take reputation, opportunity, possessions, freedom, even life, but they cannot take Christ and they cannot cancel His promise.

Paul says, “This light momentary affliction is preparing for us an eternal weight of glory beyond all comparison.” (2 Cor 4:17) Remember the man saying it: beaten, shipwrecked, imprisoned, stoned and left for dead. He is not trivialising suffering. He is comparing it with glory.

The surrounding culture may reject the citizen of the kingdom, but it cannot overturn the verdict of the King.


8. Living now by the culture of the kingdom
There is one feature of these Beatitudes that ties the whole passage together. Go back to v 3: “Blessed are the poor in spirit, for theirs is the kingdom of heaven.” Present tense: “theirs is.” Now look at v 10: “Blessed are those who are persecuted for righteousness' sake, for theirs is the kingdom of heaven.”

Again, “theirs is.”

Those two statements frame the Beatitudes. The kingdom is theirs now.

But look at the promises in between: “They shall be comforted.” “They shall inherit the earth.” “They shall be satisfied.” “They shall receive mercy.” “They shall see God.”
Future. That gives us the shape of Christian life.

The kingdom has come, and the kingdom is coming.

Jesus has arrived. The King is here. His reign has begun. People are entering His kingdom now, and yet we still pray, “Your kingdom come,” because what has begun has not yet been completed.

We mourn now, but comfort is coming. We hunger now, but satisfaction is coming. We make peace now in a world of conflict, but peace is coming. We seek purity now while still battling sin, but we will see God. We may suffer now, but the kingdom already belongs to us.

This is the “already and the not yet.” We have eternal life now, and we await resurrection. We are adopted as God's children, and Paul says we still wait eagerly for “the redemption of our bodies.” (Rom 8:23) Christ is King now, and we await the day when every knee bows and every tongue confesses that Jesus Christ is Lord. (Phil 2:10-11)

Paul says in Phil 3:20, “Our citizenship is in heaven.” That does not mean our great hope is escaping earth and living in the clouds forever. The same Bible promises resurrection and renewed creation, and Jesus has already told us the kingdom is at hand. It tells us where our ultimate allegiance lies.

We are living here now under the reign of a King whose kingdom has already broken into the present, while we wait for that reign to be seen in its fullness.

That means sometimes we experience spiritual culture shock. Things everybody around us takes for granted begin looking strange when viewed through Jesus' kingdom. Revenge looks normal until you hear, “Blessed are the merciful.” Self-promotion looks normal until you hear, “Blessed are the meek.” Protecting appearances looks normal until you hear, “Blessed are the pure in heart.” Winning at all costs looks normal until you hear, “Blessed are the peacemakers.”

And perhaps the longer we live in the culture of Jesus' kingdom, the stranger some of our old assumptions should begin to feel.


Conclusion: Which culture has shaped us?
So step back and look at the whole picture: poor in spirit, mourning, meek, hungering and thirsting for righteousness, merciful, pure in heart, peacemakers, faithful under persecution.

These are not eight different types of Christian. Jesus isn't saying some of us specialise in meekness while others specialise in mercy. This is the culture of the kingdom working itself into a people.

And perhaps the most uncomfortable question in this passage is not, “Which Beatitude do I like best?” It is: Which kingdom's culture has shaped me?

Culture gets into us almost without asking permission. If you live somewhere long enough, you stop noticing the accent. You stop noticing the customs. It just becomes normal.

And we have lived in the kingdoms of this world for a long time. We have absorbed things.

The world says, “Blessed are the self-sufficient.” Jesus says, “Blessed are the poor in spirit.”
The world says, “Hide weakness.” Jesus says, “Blessed are those who mourn.” 
The world says, “Push yourself forward.”  Jesus says, “Blessed are the meek.” 
The world says, “Hunger for success and recognition.” Jesus says, “Blessed are those who hunger and thirst for righteousness.” 
The world says, “Make people pay.” Jesus says, “Blessed are the merciful.” 
The world says, “Manage the image.” Jesus says, “Blessed are the pure in heart.” 
The world says, “Win the argument.”  Jesus says, “Blessed are the peacemakers.” 
The world says, “If your beliefs make you unpopular, change them.”  Jesus says, “Blessed are you when others revile you and persecute you... on my account.”

These are different kingdoms.

We need to be careful not to carry the passport of one kingdom while living completely by the culture of another.

G.K. Chesterton once wrote, “The Christian ideal has not been tried and found wanting. It has been found difficult; and left untried.”

It is easy to admire the Beatitudes. They look lovely on a poster. 
It is much harder when somebody actually hurts me and Jesus says, “Mercy.” 
When I want recognition and Jesus says, “Meek.” 
When everybody else says something is normal and Jesus says, “Righteousness.” 
When winning the argument feels satisfying and Jesus says, “Peacemaker.”

The kingdom is not merely something we admire. It is something we enter.

And it begins exactly where Jesus began: “Blessed are the poor in spirit.”

Empty hands.

If we hear the Beatitudes properly, none of us should reach the end and say, “I've nailed that.” Poor in spirit? Sometimes pride still runs deep. Mourning over sin? Sometimes I excuse mine rather than grieve it. Meek? Sometimes I want to win. Hungry for righteousness? Sometimes I hunger more strongly for comfort. Merciful? Until somebody hurts me. Pure in heart? God sees what nobody else sees. Peacemaker? Sometimes being right feels more attractive than making peace.

So where do we go?
Back to the first Beatitude: “Blessed are the poor in spirit.” ~Lord, I need You.
And that is the good news. The King who gives these words is the same King who gives grace.
Matthew's Gospel will eventually take us from this mountain to another hill. Jesus will not simply teach the culture of the kingdom. He will embody it. He is meek. He is merciful. He is pure. He is the Peacemaker. He hungers for His Father's will. He is persecuted for righteousness. People will revile Him, falsely accuse Him and reject Him, and He will go to the cross.
The King will give Himself for His rebellious subjects.
And then He will rise.
So the Beatitudes are not detached moral instructions. They belong to Jesus. The kingdom is at hand because the King is here.
And when people come under His reign, He begins teaching them a new normal, a new culture. Slowly, sometimes painfully, often imperfectly, but genuinely.
He takes proud people and teaches them dependence. He comforts mourners. He turns fighters into people who can be meek. He awakens hunger for righteousness. He teaches forgiven sinners to show mercy. He cleans the heart. He turns enemies into peacemakers. He gives courage to remain faithful when loyalty to Him costs something.
That is the kingdom.
And one day there will be no clash of cultures anymore. Those who mourn will be comforted. The meek will inherit the earth. Those who hunger and thirst for righteousness will be satisfied. The pure in heart will see God.
Rev 11:15 puts it like this: “The kingdom of the world has become the kingdom of our Lord and of his Christ.”
So Jesus' announcement still stands: “Repent, for the kingdom of heaven is at hand.”
The kingdom is at hand because the King is here.
And perhaps the question the Beatitudes leave us with tonight is not simply, “Do I believe in the kingdom?”
It is: Which kingdom's culture am I actually learning to live in?
Because where Jesus reigns, He creates a different kind of people. Those people may sometimes look poor. They may mourn. They may appear to lose. They may be rejected.
But Jesus looks at them and says, “Blessed.”
Because the final verdict over their lives does not belong to the culture around them.
It belongs to the King.
