The King on a Donkey
Matthew 21:1–11
Big Idea: Jesus is the promised King, but the donkey shows us what kind of King He is: humble, peaceful and coming to save by giving Himself.

Introduction
Most of us in Britain have a fairly clear idea what a royal procession is supposed to look like.
In May 2023, when King Charles III was crowned, there were state coaches, Household Cavalry, military processions, uniforms, flags, trumpets, ceremony and crowds lining the route. After the Coronation, the King and Queen travelled back to Buckingham Palace in the Gold State Coach, drawn by eight horses and surrounded by all the visible signs of monarchy.
That is what we do when we want people to know:
The King is here.
Matthew 21 also gives us a royal procession.
There is a crowd, people lining the road, shouts of acclamation.
There are garments spread before Him, language about the Son of David.

There is a city asking what is happening.


And at the centre of it all is the King.
But He is sitting on a donkey.

And if you don't understand the donkey, I think you can miss the whole scene.

Because the donkey is not an unfortunate detail. Jesus hasn't turned up to His own royal procession and thought, “Well, this was the only transport available.”

Jesus arranged this, He sent the disciples to get the animals, He chose how He would enter Jerusalem.

He knew exactly what He was doing.
And Matthew tells us why. This was fulfilment.

So tonight I want us to look at this question:
!What kind of King rides into His capital on a donkey?
Because Matthew's answer is: This King does.
And the donkey doesn't deny His kingship; It defines it.

1. The King Has Come
Read Matthew 21:1–5.

Jesus and the disciples are approaching Jerusalem.

They have come through Jericho. They arrive at Bethphage, on the Mount of Olives, and Jesus sends two disciples ahead.

He tells them that they will find a donkey tied there with her colt. They are to untie them and bring them to Him. If anyone challenges them, they are to say:

“The Lord needs them.”

Notice something before we even get to the prophecy.
!Jesus is in control of this.

Sometimes we picture the triumphal entry as though Jesus happens to approach Jerusalem, somebody finds Him a donkey, the crowd gets excited and suddenly events run away with Him.
That isn't Matthew's picture at all, He initiates this;
Jesus gives the instructions. Jesus chooses the manner of His arrival.Jesus is deliberately doing something.

Then Matthew tells us: “This took place to fulfil what was spoken through the prophet.”

And he brings together language particularly from Zechariah 9:9, with an echo of Isaiah 62:11:  “Say to Daughter Zion, ‘See, your king comes to you, gentle and riding on a donkey.’”
There is the key phrase:  Your King comes to you.

This isn't just about an unusual method of transport - Jesus is making a claim.
Israel has been waiting, God had made promises to David. God had spoken through the prophets.

There would be a King.

And now Jesus rides towards Jerusalem in a way that deliberately draws attention to that promise.

We sometimes talk about fulfilled prophecy as though it is mainly a prediction game.

A prophet said a particular thing hundreds of years earlier, Jesus did that thing, tick the box, prophecy fulfilled.
There is truth in that, but Matthew means something richer. 

It isn't just: Zechariah predicted the right animal.
It is: The King Zechariah promised has arrived.
The whole Gospel has been moving towards this.
Go right back to Matthew 1:1:
Matthew introduces Jesus as: “Jesus the Messiah, the son of David.”

That is almost the first thing Matthew wants you to know about Him.
Son of David, Royal language, the promised line, the promised King.

Then throughout Matthew's Gospel the question keeps surfacing:
Who is this man?
•    He teaches with authority.
•    He forgives sins.
•    He commands storms.
•    He casts out demons.
•    He heals the sick.
•    He raises the dead.

Peter finally says: “You are the Messiah, the Son of the living God.”

And now, as Jesus approaches Jerusalem, He does something He has often avoided before.
He makes His identity spectacularly public. He enters Jerusalem as King.
This is not the crowd forcing a title onto Jesus that He does not want.

Jesus has arranged the whole thing.

He is saying: The King has come.
But then you look at Him. And He is on a donkey.

2. Look at the Donkey
We can get this wrong in two opposite directions.

One mistake is to say: “Kings rode horses. Poor people rode donkeys. So Jesus isn't really presenting Himself as a king.”

That's not right.

In the Old Testament, donkeys and mules were not merely the transport of the poor. They could be associated with rulers, royal households and people of status.

In 2 Sam 16:2, Ziba tells David that the donkeys are there “for the king’s household to ride on.”
In 2 Saml 13:29, the king’s sons each mount their mule.
In 2 Sam 18:9, Absalom, David’s son and rival claimant to the throne, is riding a mule.

Even earlier, in Judges 10:4, Jair’s thirty sons ride thirty donkeys, and in Judges 12:14, Abdon’s sons and grandsons ride seventy donkeys. In those settings, the animals are signs of status and standing, not poverty.

And then there is the clearest royal example of all.

When David wanted Solomon publicly recognised as king in 1 Kings 1, he gave a very specific instruction.

Solomon was to ride the king’s own mule.

That is how he was presented to the people.
So nobody who knew Israel's Scriptures needed to look at Jesus on a donkey and think: “Well, obviously not a king then.”

Quite the opposite.

A royal rider on a donkey or mule already had biblical precedent.
And Zechariah explicitly says: “Your king comes to you.”

So the donkey is part of the royal identification.

But the other mistake is to notice the royal association and therefore lose the humility.
Because Zechariah does not merely say: “Your King is coming.”
He says He comes gentle, humble, riding on a donkey.

So the donkey doesn't tell us that Jesus isn't King.
It tells us what kind of King He is.

The donkey doesn't deny His kingship. It defines it.
And what kind of King is this? A gentle King. A humble King.

A King who does not need to swagger.
A King whose authority is so complete that He does not have to constantly prove that He has it.

There is an enormous difference between humility and weakness.
Jesus is not weak in this passage.
Look at Him. He knows where He is going. He knows what He is doing. He gives instructions. He fulfils the prophecy deliberately. He rides into the capital.He receives the cries of the crowd.

There is nothing timid about Jesus here.
This is authority without swagger.
We often confuse strength with display; the louder somebody is, the stronger they must be.
The more they insist on being respected, the more authority they must have.
The more impressive the car, the office, the title, the clothes, the platform, the entourage, the more important the person must be.
Jesus needs none of it; He knows who He is;  He knows where He came from.He knows where He is going.

So He can ride a donkey.

And this isn't new. Earlier in Matthew Jesus said: “I am gentle and humble in heart.” (11:29)

Jesus doesn't merely teach humility. He is humble.
Jesus doesn't merely describe the values of His kingdom. He embodies them.
We heard this back in the Sermon on the Mount: “Blessed are the meek.”
Well, here is the King of that kingdom. And He is meek.
And yet He is anything but powerless.
That matters because sometimes we imagine meekness as weakness with a Christian label stuck on it.
Biblical meekness isn't inability. It is strength that does not need to dominate.
Power under control. Authority without self-exaltation.

And Zechariah takes us further.
Zechariah 9:10 continues the picture.
This King cuts off the chariot, He removes the war horse, The battle bow is broken, And He proclaims peace to the nations, His rule extends from sea to sea.

So the donkey belongs to a bigger picture.

This King is bringing a kingdom unlike the kingdoms of this world.
He doesn't simply defeat one army and replace one empire with another. His kingdom reaches the nations. His reign brings peace.

And His victory comes in a way nobody standing on that road yet fully understands.

There is an interesting contemporary connection here.

For all the visible splendour surrounding the 2023 Coronation, the official theme of the Coronation service was “Called to Serve.”
That instinct is profoundly Christian. Authority is meant to serve.

But with Jesus it goes infinitely further.
Just a few verses before our passage, in Matthew 20:28, Jesus has told His disciples: “The Son of Man did not come to be served, but to serve, and to give his life as a ransom for many.”
Then almost immediately Matthew says: Here comes the King.
Do you see the connection?
What kind of King? The King who gives Himself, The King who serves, The King who gives His life as a ransom.

So don't look at the donkey and think Jesus is less of a King, Look at the donkey and learn what His kingship is like.

3. Go Out to Meet the King
Read Matthew 21:6–9.
The disciples bring the donkey and the colt. They place their cloaks on them and Jesus sits on the cloaks.

Then the crowd begins to respond. Some spread their cloaks on the road. Others cut branches from the trees and spread them on the road.

Again, there is royal imagery here.

In 2 Kings 9:13, when Jehu is proclaimed king, people take their garments and spread them beneath him.
So imagine the scene. Jesus is approaching Jerusalem. People are coming out. The road becomes a place of welcome.
Garments go down. Branches go down.
People move ahead of Him and behind Him. And the noise rises.
This is the arrival of a King. There is an idea from elsewhere in the New Testament which helps us picture what royal arrival could look like.
Paul uses a Greek word in 1 Thessalonians 4:17 when he talks about the return of Jesus.
The word is apantēsis, ἀπάντησις.

It means a meeting, going out to meet someone who is arriving.

In the ancient world, that language could be used in the setting of a dignitary approaching a city. People would go out to meet the arriving figure and accompany him on the final part of his arrival.
We see something similar in Acts 28:15. Christians from Rome hear that Paul is approaching, so they travel out from Rome to meet him. They meet him on the road and then accompany him towards Rome.

Jesus uses the same noun in the parable of the ten virgins in Matthew 25:6: “The bridegroom is coming. Come out to meet him.”

And Paul takes that language in 1 Thessalonians 4 and applies it on the greatest possible scale.

The Lord is coming. His people go out to meet Him.

Now, one caution. The word apantēsis itself doesn't automatically mean, every single time, “go out and escort a king back into the city”. Its basic sense is “meeting”. But the wider civic pattern of going out to welcome an arriving dignitary is real, and it gives a powerful background to Paul's language.

And before anyone starts drawing an end-times chart, that's not tonight's sermon.

The point I want you to see is much simpler. When the King is coming, His people go to meet Him.

You don't go out because the King has become lost and needs somebody to show him where the city is.
You go out because his arrival matters, His arrival claims attention, His arrival demands a response.

You go out to welcome him.
John's account of this same entry actually says that the crowd heard Jesus was coming to Jerusalem and went out to meet Him.
That is what is happening here. Jerusalem is confronted by its King.

And this is also where Matthew 21 begins to reach beyond Palm Sunday.
Because Jesus will come again. The King who once approached Jerusalem on a donkey will not remain rejected forever.

Paul says the Lord Himself will descend. 
The dead in Christ will rise.
Those who are His will be gathered to Him.
And we will be with the Lord forever.

The first arrival and the final arrival are not identical.
But the same Jesus is King in both.

And the appropriate response to the coming King is welcome.
The question is: Do we actually want this King?
Not a Jesus we have redesigned, or simply approves whatever we already believe.
Not a Jesus who fits neatly into our politics, or gives us religious inspiration but never commands us.
Not a Jesus who saves but never rules. The Jesus who arrives here is King.

And you don't welcome a king by saying: “Lovely to see you. Just don't interfere with anything.”
A king has a claim. Jesus has a claim.

4. “Hosanna to the Son of David”
Listen to the crowd:“Hosanna to the Son of David!” “Blessed is he who comes in the name of the Lord!”“Hosanna in the highest heaven!”

They are drawing from Psalm 118.
And that word Hosanna is important.

We tend to use Hosanna almost as another word for “Praise God!” And by the first century it certainly carried that celebratory sense.

But underneath it is a cry: Save, please. Save now.

Think about that.
Jesus is riding into Jerusalem.
The people are crying: Save us!

And that is exactly what He has come to do.
They understand more than they know.
“Hosanna!” Save us.
Jesus says: Yes.

But the salvation I am bringing is deeper than you yet understand.
Because what happens next?

He doesn't ride to a palace and take a throne. He rides towards a cross.

There is the great tension of this passage. The crowd is right.
He is the Son of David. He does come in the name of the Lord. He is the King.
He has come to save.

But how will He save?
That is where Jesus overturns every normal expectation of power.

If we were writing the story, what would happen next?
The Messiah arrives.
The crowd rallies around Him.
The authorities fall.
The enemy is defeated.
The throne is taken.
End of story.

But Jesus has already told His disciples what will happen in Jerusalem.
back in Matthew 20:18–19. “We are going up to Jerusalem.”

The Son of Man will be delivered over.
Condemned. Mocked. Flogged. Crucified.

And on the third day raised to life.

Jesus knows exactly where this road leads.
That makes the triumphal entry much more powerful.

He is not riding into Jerusalem because He has misunderstood the danger.
He isn't caught by surprise later in the week. He rides in knowing.

Every step the donkey takes carries Him closer to the cross.
And still He goes. That is your King.
He hears the cry: “Save us.”

And He goes to save them. Not by calling down an army.
Not by slaughtering His enemies.

But by giving Himself for His enemies. At the cross, people will mock His kingship.
A sign will be placed above Him identifying Him as King.
Soldiers will give Him a robe. They will put something like a royal sceptre in His hand.
They will give Him a crown. But the crown will be thorns.
They will kneel and say: “Hail, King of the Jews.”
They think the joke is that this man clearly isn't a king.
Matthew knows the deeper truth. He is.
The crucified man is King.
And His greatest victory is being accomplished precisely where the world thinks He has lost.
This is why Christianity cannot be reduced to: “Jesus was a nice man who taught us to be nicer.”
Nor even: “Jesus gives us a really good example of humility.”

Of course He is our example.
But first He is our Saviour.
He doesn't simply show us how to die to ourselves. He dies for us.
He doesn't merely demonstrate sacrificial love. He becomes the sacrifice.

“Hosanna.” Save us.

And the answer to that prayer is a cross and an empty tomb.

5. The King They Were Waiting For, But Not Necessarily the King They Expected
There is something we need to be careful about here.
Palm Sunday sermons sometimes say: “On Sunday the crowd shouted ‘Hosanna!’ and on Friday the very same people shouted ‘Crucify Him!’”

It is a powerful line.

The problem is Matthew doesn't actually tell us that they were all the same people.

Jerusalem was full of pilgrims. There were different crowds involved at different points.

So we shouldn't accuse individual people of a hypocrisy the text doesn't establish.

But there is still a genuine tension running through the Gospel.
Jesus is the King Israel has been waiting for. Yet His kingship is repeatedly misunderstood.
Even His own disciples struggle with it.

Just before this passage James and John's mother asks for her sons to have the places of honour in Jesus' kingdom.
Jesus has to explain: “You still don't understand how My kingdom works.”
The rulers of the Gentiles lord it over people. “Not so with you.”
Whoever wants to become great must become a servant.
Then: “The Son of Man did not come to be served, but to serve.”

Then He gets on the donkey.

Matthew isn't being subtle. This is what the kingdom looks like.
The King is different, therefore His people must be different.

If our model of Christian leadership looks more like celebrity culture than Jesus, something has gone wrong.

If Christian ministry becomes a competition for platform, recognition and influence, something has gone wrong.

If I need everybody to know how important I am, I have stopped looking at the donkey.

If I cannot serve unless somebody notices, I have stopped looking at the King.

If I think leadership means that other people exist to make my life easier, Jesus says:
“Not so with you.” Our King serves. Our King stoops. Our King gives Himself.

So yes, there is an application to humility. But we must get the order right.
Christianity does not say: Be humble enough and perhaps Jesus will accept you.
It says:Look at the humble King who came to save you.
Receive Him. Trust Him.Then become like the King you follow.
Grace first. Transformation follows.

6. “Who Is This?”
Read Matthew 21:10–11. “When Jesus entered Jerusalem, the whole city was stirred.”
I love that phrase. The King arrives and the city cannot simply carry on as though nothing has happened.

Something is happening.
People are asking: “Who is this?”
That may be the most important question in the whole passage. Who is this?
And the crowd answers: “This is Jesus, the prophet from Nazareth in Galilee.”
Is that wrong? No. Jesus is a prophet.

He is Jesus of Nazareth. But is that enough? No.
Matthew has just spent the entire scene showing us that it isn't enough.
The crowd says: Prophet.
Matthew says: King.

The crowd's answer is true, but it is inadequate.

And you can do exactly the same thing with Jesus today.
You can say: “Jesus was a great teacher.” True. Not enough. 
“Jesus taught us to love people.” True. Not enough.
“Jesus challenged hypocrisy.” True. Not enough.
“Jesus cared about the poor and the outsider.” True.Not enough.
“Jesus was a prophet.” True.Not enough.
Because the question is not: Can you say something complimentary about Jesus?
The question is: Who is this?
Matthew's answer is enormous.
This is the Messiah.
The Son of David.
The Son of God.
The promised King.
The Lord.
The one who came to save His people from their sins.

And if that is who He is, neutrality disappears.
You can admire a teacher and then ignore him.
You can appreciate a philosopher and choose another philosophy.
You can respect a prophet and keep your distance.
But a King confronts you with a different question.

Will you receive His rule?
That is where this passage becomes uncomfortable.

Because most of us quite like the idea of Jesus as Saviour.
Jesus forgives me. Jesus loves me. Jesus helps me. Jesus comforts me.Jesus will take me to heaven. Lovely.
But Matthew shows Him as King. And kings rule. The Jesus who saves you also has a claim over you.
Over your relationships. money. ambitions. sexuality. temper. grudges. politics. plans. Time.
Over the parts of life you would quite like Christianity to leave alone.
The city asks: “Who is this?”
That is not merely a first-century question. It is tonight's question.

Who is Jesus to you?  Not Your parents. Not  your church. Not what box would you tick on a census form.
Who is this?
7. The King Rides Towards the Cross
Let us bring the pieces together.
The King has come. The donkey tells us what kind of King He is.
The crowd goes out to welcome Him. They cry: “Hosanna.” Save us.
The city asks: “Who is this?”

And Jesus keeps riding. Where?
Towards the cross.

This is what makes Christian kingship so utterly different
Jesus does not become King because He manages to avoid suffering.

His path to glory runs through suffering.

Paul later describes the same movement in Philippians 2.
Christ humbled Himself. He took the nature of a servant. He became obedient to death.Even death on a cross. Therefore God exalted Him.

The pattern is humility, obedience, cross, exaltation.
That isn't accidental. It reveals the heart of God.

Sometimes people imagine the Father as angry and Jesus as the kind one who persuades Him to forgive us.
That isn't the Gospel. God so loved the world that He gave His Son.
The Son willingly gives Himself. The Spirit applies that salvation to us.
The cross is not Jesus rescuing us from an unwilling God. It is God acting in love to save sinners.
Your King came for you. And He came knowing the cost.
The donkey is therefore not just a symbol of humility in general.It points towards the whole direction of His mission.
Down. He comes down. He serves. He stoops. He washes feet. He gives Himself. He goes to the cross. And then God raises Him.

This is why the world so often misunderstands Christian power.
The world says: If you are powerful, make others serve you.
Jesus says: I am Lord, and I serve.
The world says: If somebody opposes you, crush them. Jesus prays for His executioners.
The world says: Save yourself. 
At the cross they literally shout versions of that at Him. “If you are the Son of God, come down.”
But He doesn't. Why? 
Because if He saves Himself from the cross, He does not save us through the cross.
The crowd cried: “Hosanna.” And staying on the cross is how He answers.

8. Don't Mistake Humility for Lack of Authority
There is one final balance we need.
We can preach the gentle Jesus so softly that He becomes harmless.
The Jesus of Matthew 21 is humble. But He is not harmless.
Look at what happens immediately after this passage.
He enters the temple. He overturns tables.

He drives out those who have turned His Father's house into something it should never have become.

Gentle does not mean indifferent to evil. Humble does not mean unwilling to confront sin.

And the New Testament also tells us that the Jesus who came in humility will return in glory.

Revelation eventually gives us another mounted picture of Jesus, this time on a white horse, coming as the righteous Judge and victorious King.
So do not misunderstand the donkey.He is saying:“This is what I do with My authority.”
He uses it to save. He uses it to serve. He uses it in perfect obedience to the Father.
He uses it in righteousness. But He remains King.
Zechariah said His rule would extend from sea to sea.
At the end of Matthew's Gospel Jesus will say: “All authority in heaven and on earth has been given to me.”

Then He sends His disciples to all nations.

Zechariah saw the King whose reign would reach the nations.
Matthew ends with the risen King sending His people to the nations.

The donkey wasn't the end of the kingdom.
It was its revelation.
Conclusion
So come back to the road into Jerusalem.,  See the crowd.See the cloaks. Hear the branches moving. Hear the shouting.
“Hosanna!”
“Son of David!”

“Blessed is He who comes in the name of the Lord!”

Then look towards the centre.
There He is. The King. On a donkey.
Don't be embarrassed by the donkey. Don't explain it away.
Look at it. Because it tells you something about your King.
He is powerful enough to be humble. He is secure enough to serve.
He is holy enough to confront evil. He is loving enough to give Himself.
He is King enough not to need the world's symbols of power. And He has come to save.
The donkey doesn't deny His kingship. It defines it.
And hear the cry:
Hosanna. Save us.
That is exactly what He came to do.
The King has come.
And the donkey tells us what kind of King He is.
