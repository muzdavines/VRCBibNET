Behold the Lamb of God
John 1:19–34
Last week Aaron opened up John 1:1–18 for us and described it as the overture to the Gospel. I think that is exactly right. Before the story really gets moving, John has already sounded the great themes we will hear again and again: the Word, life, light, witness, glory, grace, and the Word becoming flesh.

Now, at verse 19, the overture gives way to the action. It is almost as though the movie camera starts rolling. We leave those great opening statements, and suddenly we are on the ground. Priests and Levites arrive from Jerusalem, asking questions; John answers them, and somewhere in the scene, Jesus is already standing there.

And for this passage, I want us to picture it another way as well.

Imagine the world as a great painting. God is not merely looking at it from outside. The Painter has stepped into His own painting.

That is the astonishing claim John has already made in the opening verses. The One through whom everything was made has entered the world He made.

And now comes the irony. The Painter is standing inside the painting, and people do not recognise Him.

John the Baptist says in verse 26, “Among you stands one you don't know.”

Jesus is already there.

So John the Baptist's job is very simple. He is not the centre of the picture. He is the man standing beside the canvas saying, “Look there.”

And there is one word in this passage that really captures his ministry. Verse 29:

“Behold.” Look. Pay attention. There He is.

And as John points to Jesus, he tells us three enormously important things about Him. He is the Lamb of God who takes away sin. He is the One who baptises with the Holy Spirit. He is the Son of God.

So last week the question was largely, “Who is Jesus?”
Now John begins to answer, “Why has He come, and what has He come to do?”

Don’t look at me — John 1:19–28
Verse 19 says, “And this is the testimony of John, when the Jews sent priests and Levites from Jerusalem to ask him, ‘Who are you?’”

John has become difficult to ignore. Crowds are coming out to him. He's preaching repentance. He's baptising people. Something is happening in the wilderness, and the religious authorities in Jerusalem want to know what it is.

So they send a delegation. And their first question is, “Who are you?”

John's answer is striking. Verse 20 says, “He confessed, and did not deny, but confessed, ‘I am not the Christ.’” John is almost falling over himself to make the point. I am not the Messiah.

And then they keep going. “What then? Are you Elijah?” John says, “I am not.”

Now we need to be a bit careful here because Jesus later speaks of John as fulfilling the expectation of Elijah. Malachi had promised that Elijah would come before the day of the Lord, and Luke tells us that John comes “in the spirit and power of Elijah.” So John isn't denying that he fulfils that prophetic role. John denies being Elijah in the sense the delegation is asking. Yet Jesus later identifies John’s ministry as fulfilling the expectation of Elijah, and Luke tells us that he came ‘in the spirit and power of Elijah’. 

Then they ask, “Are you the Prophet?” Again John says no. That almost certainly reaches back to Deut. 18 where Moses says that God will raise up a prophet like him.

So look at what is happening. They keep offering John increasingly impressive identities. Are you the Messiah? No. Elijah? No. The Prophet? No.

Eventually they're almost exasperated. “Who are you? We need to give an answer to those who sent us.”

And John finally tells them: “I am the voice of one crying out in the wilderness, ‘Make straight the way of the Lord.’”

And I love the contrast. Last week (Aaron) John the Gospel writer introduced us to the Word. John the Baptist says, “I am only a voice.”

A voice exists to communicate something beyond itself. A voice is heard, delivers its message, and passes. John TB isn't interested in building a monument to John. He has a job. Prepare the road. Point to Christ.

That is healthy Christian witness. And it is healthy Christian leadership. The temptation in any form of ministry is for the messenger gradually to become the message. People can become attached to personalities. Preachers can enjoy being listened to. Leaders can begin to think the church depends on them.

And John cuts straight through that: “I am not the Christ.”

That is probably a sentence every Christian leader should remember. The church doesn't belong to us. The gospel is not about us. We cannot save anybody. We cannot change anybody's heart. At our best, we are voices saying, “Look there.”

And John is so determined to point away from himself that when Jesus finally appears, his instinct is almost, “Stop looking at me now. There He is.”

The delegation has another question. If you're not the Messiah, Elijah or the Prophet, why are you baptising?

And that is not an unreasonable question. Various Jewish washings already existed. Ritual cleansing was familiar. But John is baptising Jews in preparation for God's coming kingdom. That carries a sting. He is effectively saying, “Don't assume that because you belong to Israel, everything is fine. You need repentance. You need cleansing. You need to prepare for the Lord.”

And John answers, “I baptise with water, but among you stands one you don't know.”

We'll come back to the baptism contrast because John hasn't finished with it. But notice the second half: “Among you stands one you don't know.”

That sentence ought to bother us a little. Because these aren't people who know nothing about God. They're priests. Levites. Religious people. They know Scripture. They know worship. They know the traditions. And yet the One to whom all of it points is standing among them, and they don't recognise Him.

You can know an awful lot about Christianity while missing Christ. You can know the hymns. Know the Bible stories. Know the church calendar. Know the language. Know when to stand and when to sit. And still the question remains: do you know the One all of this is about?

Jesus later says, “You search the Scriptures because you think that in them you have eternal life; and it is they that bear witness about me.”

The Scriptures are not the problem. Knowing Scripture isn't the problem. The tragedy is reading all the signposts and never arriving at the destination.

John is saying, “He's here.”

And then John says, “He who comes after me, the strap of whose sandal I am not worthy to untie.”

John isn't pretending that his own calling is insignificant. God sent him. God gave him a message. God gave him a ministry. But John knows the difference between the servant and the Lord.

And that prepares us for the next morning. Because finally, John sees Him.


2. Behold the Lamb - John 1:29
“The next day he saw Jesus coming toward him, and said, ‘Behold, the Lamb of God, who takes away the sin of the world!’”

There is the moment. Behold. Look.

John has spent the first part of the passage saying, “Don’t look at me.” Now he tells us where to look. And his first great description of Jesus is remarkable: “The Lamb of God.”

Not first the teacher. Not first the miracle worker. Not first the king. John says, “The Lamb.”

And right at the beginning of Jesus’ public ministry, the language of sacrifice appears. We’re nowhere near the crucifixion chronologically, yet already the cross is beginning to cast its shadow across the Gospel.

When John calls Jesus “the Lamb of God”, I don’t think we necessarily have to choose one single Old Testament passage and say, “That is the one John means.” There are several great strands running through Scripture, and when John points at Jesus, they seem to come together.

Go back first to Genesis 22. Abraham is climbing Mount Moriah with Isaac. Isaac notices something. There is wood. There is fire. But something is missing. He asks his father, “Behold, the fire and the wood, but where is the lamb for a burnt offering?”

And Abraham gives that extraordinary answer: “God will provide for himself the lamb.”

In Genesis 22 God does provide. Isaac doesn’t die. A ram is provided in his place. And from very early in Scripture we are being taught something: when sacrifice is needed, God provides what His people cannot provide for themselves.

That is important because our instinct is so often to ask, “What can I do to put this right?” How can I make myself acceptable? How can I make up for what I have done? But the direction of the gospel is the other way around. God provides.

And now John sees Jesus walking towards him and says, “Behold, the Lamb of God.”

Then there is the Passover in Exodus 12. Israel is enslaved in Egypt. Judgement is coming. God tells each household to take a lamb. The lamb dies, its blood marks the house, judgment passes over, and the people walk out of slavery.

A lamb dies, and a people go free.

And John's Gospel will later make the Passover setting around Jesus' death very difficult to miss. John wants us thinking in those categories: judgment, deliverance, sacrifice, blood, freedom. God rescuing His people.

But there is something even bigger happening here. At Passover, each household sheltered under the blood of the lamb, and Israel was delivered from judgement and slavery. 

Then there is the whole sacrificial system. Sacrifices are offered again and again, year after year. And every sacrifice teaches the same uncomfortable truth: sin is serious.

We often soften sin into mistakes, bad choices, weaknesses or things we need to improve. Scripture goes deeper. Sin is rebellion against God. It separates us from Him. Reconciliation is costly. Life is given. Blood is shed.

And yet the repetition of those sacrifices tells us something as well. They keep having to be offered.

If one sacrifice had finally removed the problem, there would have been no need for another one.

Hebrews later says plainly, “It is impossible for the blood of bulls and goats to take away sins.”

So the sacrifices are doing two things. They are teaching Israel how serious sin is, but they are also creating expectation. They are pointing beyond themselves. Something more is needed.

And then Isaiah 53 brings the picture into even sharper focus.

“All we like sheep have gone astray; we have turned, every one, to his own way.”

That is the human problem. We are the wandering sheep.

But then Isaiah says, “The LORD has laid on him the iniquity of us all.”

And a few verses later, “Like a lamb that is led to the slaughter... he opened not his mouth.”

Now notice the movement. In Genesis, God provides. At Passover, a lamb dies and people go free. In the sacrifices, we learn that sin requires atonement, but none of those sacrifices is the final answer. In Isaiah, the servant bears the sins of others and goes like a lamb to slaughter. 

In the sacrifices, we learn that sin requires atonement, but none of those sacrifices is the final answer.

In Isaiah, the servant bears the sins of others and goes like a lamb to slaughter.

Then John looks up.  Jesus is walking towards him, 
And he says: “Behold, the Lamb of God.”

It is almost as though all those lines that have been running through Scripture suddenly meet in one person.

God has provided. The greater deliverance is coming.

The One who will bear the sins of others is here.

John points to Him as the Lamb of God.

God's provision. - His answer to the problem those sacrifices could never finally resolve. 

He has come to deal with the thing we cannot deal with ourselves. Sin.

And that takes us straight into the heart of the gospel, and into the rest of John’s sentence:

“Behold, the Lamb of God, who takes away the sin of the world.”


Who takes away the sin of the world?
John is telling us what the Lamb does: “who takes away the sin of the world.”

The verb carries the sense of lifting, carrying, removing. And notice the singular. Of course there are individual sins, things we do, say and fail to do. But John gathers the whole human problem under that one great heading: sin. 

Obviously there are individual sins. Things we say. Things we do. Things we fail to do. But underneath all those individual sins lies something deeper: sin.

A broken relationship with our Creator. A bent away from God. A desire to live God's world on our terms.

And that means our greatest problem is not simply outside us.

If you remove every difficult circumstance from my life, I still bring Paul with me.

If you put me in perfect surroundings, the human heart is still the human heart.

The problem is not merely education. It's not merely poverty. It's not merely politics. It's not merely bad influences. All of those things matter, but Scripture diagnoses something deeper.

Humanity needs reconciliation with God.

And John says, “Here is the One who deals with it.”

That means Following Christ  is fundamentally different from self-improvement.

Self-improvement says, “Try harder. Become more disciplined. Correct your weaknesses.” And some of that can be useful. But you cannot self-improve your way into reconciliation with a holy God.

You cannot scrub sin away. You cannot undo guilt by accumulating enough good deeds. You cannot paint over the damaged part of the canvas and call it restored.

And this is where our picture from last week still helps us without needing to repeat the whole sermon.

The Painter has entered the damaged painting.

But He has not entered merely to stand at a safe distance from the damage and give instructions. He enters it. He comes as the Lamb. Ultimately, He bears the cost of restoring what His creatures have ruined.

That's why the cross isn't an unfortunate ending to an otherwise successful ministry. The cross is where the direction of this verse is taking us.

“Behold, the Lamb of God, who takes away the sin of the world.”

And don't miss those last three words: “of the world.”

John is speaking in Israel. The Messiah comes through Israel. The imagery comes from Israel's Scriptures. But already John's horizon is bigger than Israel alone.

The world!!.

That word is going to echo through the Gospel. John 3:16: “For God so loved the world...” John 4, after the Samaritans meet Jesus: “We know that this is indeed the Saviour of the world.”

Jesus has not come as a tribal saviour. He's not merely Israel's answer to Roman occupation. The problem He has come to address is universal. Sin is universal. And so the gospel goes to the world.

That doesn't mean every person is automatically saved. John's Gospel will repeatedly call for faith. But it does mean nobody can say, “He's not for people like me.”

Jew. Gentile. Religious. Irreligious. Respectable. Broken. Near. Far away.

John points to Jesus and says, “The Lamb of God who takes away the sin of the world.”


“He was before me” — John 1:30–31
John then says, “After me comes a man who ranks before me, because he was before me.”

We don't need to spend long there because John has already told us why. We dealt with that last week. John the Baptist was born before Jesus, yet Jesus “was before” John because Jesus' existence did not begin at Bethlehem.

The man approaching John is the eternal Word.

But then John says something interesting: “I myself did not know him.”

That probably doesn't mean John had literally never met Jesus. The Gospels give us family connections. The point is that John did not know Him in this revealed messianic identity until God revealed it to him.

John goes on: “but for this purpose I came baptising with water, that he might be revealed to Israel.”

Even John's baptism ministry is ultimately about revealing Jesus.

Again, John won't let us make him the centre.

Why are you baptising, John? So that He might be revealed.

Why are you preaching, John? So that people are ready for Him.

Why are you here? To point to Christ.


The Spirit descended and remained – John 1:32–33
Then John tells us what God showed him: “I saw the Spirit descend from heaven like a dove, and it remained on him.”

There is one little word there which matters: remained.

The Spirit descended and remained on Jesus.

John uses that idea of remaining or abiding repeatedly throughout his Gospel. And here, at the beginning of Jesus' ministry, the Spirit rests upon Him.

That has deep Old Testament roots. Isaiah 11 speaks of the coming king: “And the Spirit of the LORD shall rest upon him.” Isaiah 42 speaks of God's servant: “I have put my Spirit upon him.” Isaiah 61 says, “The Spirit of the Lord GOD is upon me, because the LORD has anointed me to bring good news.”

John is telling us: the promised Spirit-anointed One has arrived.

And then comes something even greater. John says God told him, “He on whom you see the Spirit descend and remain, this is he who baptises with the Holy Spirit.”

Now the contrast becomes clear.

John baptises with water. Jesus baptises with the Holy Spirit.

John can administer a sign. Jesus can give the reality to which it points. John can immerse somebody in water. Jesus can give the Spirit of God.

That is an extraordinary claim, because throughout the Old Testament, giving the Spirit is something God promises to do.

Ezekiel 36 says, “I will put my Spirit within you.”

Joel says, “I will pour out my Spirit on all flesh.”

And now John says of Jesus, “He baptises with the Holy Spirit.”

Think about how high a claim that is. Jesus isn't merely someone who has the Spirit. He gives the Spirit.

In Ezekiel, Joel and the prophets, God says “I will give My Spirit”. Yet John identifies Jesus as the One who does it. So “He baptises with the Holy Spirit” does two things at once: it tells us what Jesus does and reveals who Jesus is. 

The gospel isn’t “Jesus forgives your past and then leaves you to manufacture the Christian life yourself.” The same Christ who removes guilt gives the Spirit for new life. And this matters because sometimes we reduce salvation to only one side of it.

We rightly talk about forgiveness. We rightly talk about guilt being dealt with. But Jesus does more than bring forgiveness.

The Lamb takes away sin. And the Spirit brings new life.

Those belong together.

God doesn't say, “I'll forgive you, now off you go and try a bit harder.” The gospel is not simply pardon followed by self-effort.

Jesus deals with the guilt of sin and gives His people the Spirit. And that's why the Christian life is possible at all.

Not because following Christ suddenly makes people naturally brilliant. Not because temptation disappears. Not because we never struggle. But because God does something within His people.

Ezekiel's promise was, “I will give you a new heart, and a new spirit I will put within you.” And then, “I will put my Spirit within you, and cause you to walk in my statutes.”

That is transformation from the inside out.

Here, our painting metaphor can take one final step forward. The Painter has entered the painting. The damage is real. He comes as the Lamb to deal with sin. But He doesn't merely clean up the surface. He begins new creation within His people by His Spirit.

That's considerably bigger than becoming religious, attending church, behaving yourself.

Christianity says that God gives life.

And this means we should be very careful that Following Christ does not become merely external.

We can become very good at external Christianity. Attend. Sing. Listen. Volunteer. Serve. And those things are good. But you can reproduce religious behaviour without spiritual life.

John isn't pointing us towards a better religious system. He is pointing towards Christ.

The Lamb who removes sin. The One who gives the Spirit.

So genuine Christian faith isn't simply, “I've adopted Christian values.”

It's, “I need Christ. I need His sacrifice. I need His life. I need His Spirit.”

And that's why the answer to spiritual dryness isn't merely, “I must make myself more enthusiastic.”

The answer is always to return to Christ.

Look again where John is pointing.


“This is the Son of God” — John 1:34
NIV ~ Chosen One ~ Variant

And then John brings his testimony to its conclusion: “And I have seen and have borne witness that this is the Son of God.”

Look at the movement of the passage.
 “John, who are you?” →  “Who is Jesus?”

But John spends the passage moving the question.

By verse 34 the important question is no longer, “Who is John?”

It is, “Who is Jesus?”

And John's answer is: the Lamb of God, the One who takes away sin, the One who baptises with the Holy Spirit, the Son of God.

That's John's witness.


And notice the language: “I have seen and have borne witness.”

This Gospel is going to make a lot of testimony. 

John the Baptist says, “I saw, and I bear witness.” Later, Jesus' works testify, the Scriptures testify, the Father testifies, the Spirit testifies, and eventually the disciples testify.

Why all this witness? Because John's Gospel isn't merely giving us information to admire. Testimony calls for a response. Will we believe the witness and look to the One being pointed out? 

John isn't saying, “I've developed an interesting philosophy about Jesus.”

He is saying, “I saw. God showed me. I bear witness. This is the Son of God.”


Conclusion: Look where John is pointing
So we started with a group of religious leaders asking John, “Who are you?”

And John essentially spends the whole passage saying, “You're looking in the wrong direction.”

I'm a voice. I'm preparing the road. I'm baptising with water. But someone greater is here.

And then Jesus walks towards him.

And John says, “Behold.” ~ Look!!.

Last week we saw that the Painter has stepped into His own painting.

This week John begins to tell us why.

He has come into a world damaged by sin. And He doesn't remain safely outside the damage. He comes as the Lamb.

“Behold, the Lamb of God, who takes away the sin of the world.”

And He has not come merely to forgive us and leave us unchanged. He is the One who baptises with the Holy Spirit. He gives new life.

And John says, “This is the Son of God.”

So the question isn't simply, “Do you know about Jesus?”

The priests and Levites knew a great deal about the things of God. Jesus was standing among them and they did not know Him.

The question is: have you looked where John is pointing?

Because that's where genuine faith begins.

Not by staring at ourselves. Not by asking whether we've become good enough. Not by polishing the damaged parts of our own little corner of the painting.

John says, “Behold.”

Look away from yourself.

Look to Christ.

The Lamb God has provided, the One who takes away sin, the One who gives the Spirit, the Son of God.
